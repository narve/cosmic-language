e and π

New constants: e and π. Both are introduced by a sentence that pins them down exactly. e is the value of exp at 1: the number that the product rule makes every other value a power of.


; e is exp of 1
(= e (exp 1))

; it lies between 2 and 3
(< 2 e 3)
(< (÷ 8 3) e (÷ 11 4))
; exp of a natural number is a power of e
(∀ u ℕ (= (exp u) (^ e u)))

As the previous lesson showed, exp of i × u goes round the circle of radius 1 as u grows. It starts at 1, when u is 0. Half way round, at the opposite side of the circle, it is neg 1, and the distance travelled along the circle to get there is π. So π is half a turn: a full turn is 2π, and a quarter turn is π/2. It is the same π as the ratio of a circle's length to its width, but here it needs no circle, only exp. π is the only number between 0 and 4 for which exp of i times that number is (neg 1):

; Euler's identity: exp of i × π, plus 1, is 0
(= (+ (exp (× i π)) 1) 0)
; a full turn gives 1, a quarter turn gives i
(= (exp (× 2 i π)) 1)
(= (exp (× i (÷ π 2))) i)

; π lies between 3 and 4
(< 3 π 4)
; closer: between 223/71 and 22/7, Archimedes' bounds
(< (÷ 223 71) π (÷ 22 7))

Two infinite sums lead to the same π, found in a completely different way: they use only fractions, no exp and no i. The first adds and subtracts the fractions with odd denominators; the second adds the fractions 1 over each square, and the result involves π although nothing in it is about circles. One definition would be enough; three that agree leave little room for a wrong reading.


; π = 4 × (1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + …)
(= π (× 4 (apply + (sequence u ℕ (÷ (^ (neg 1) u) (+ (× 2 u) 1))))))

; 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + 1/16 + … = π²/6
(= (apply + (sequence u ℕ (÷ 1 (^ (+ u 1) 2)))) (÷ (^ π 2) 6))
