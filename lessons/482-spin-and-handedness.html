spin and handedness

The particles of the message turn, as if each were a tiny spinning top. New: spin, the size of that turning, which is a multiple of ħ.

; electron, proton and neutron spin with ħ/2, the photon with ħ
(observed (= (spin electron) (× (÷ 1 2) ħ)))
(observed (= (spin proton) (× (÷ 1 2) ħ)))
(observed (= (spin neutron) (× (÷ 1 2) ħ)))

(observed (= (spin photon) ħ))
; spin has the dimension of ħ
(∀ u (list electron proton neutron photon) (= (dim (spin u)) (dim ħ)))

A turning has an axis and a sense. New: turns, a particle turning from one direction toward another at right angles, and spin-axis, the direction of its axis. The axis is tied to the turning by cross, from the lesson on geometry.

; a particle that turns from v toward w has its spin axis along the
; cross of v and w
(∀ u (list electron proton neutron) (∀ v directions (∀ w directions
  (implies (and (= (dot v w) 0) (turns u v w))
           (= (spin-axis u) (cross v w))))))
; for example: turning from (1 0 0) toward (0 1 0), the axis is (0 0
; 1)
(∀ u (list electron proton neutron)
  (implies (turns u (list 1 0 0) (list 0 1 0)) (= (spin-axis u) (list 0 0 1))))
; a particle that turns from v toward w does not turn from w toward
; v
(∀ u (list electron proton neutron) (∀ v directions (∀ w directions
  (implies (turns u v w) (not (turns u w v))))))
; the spin axis is a direction
(∀ u (list electron proton neutron) (∃ v directions (= v (spin-axis u))))

Is the hand of cross only a convention? For gravity, electricity, magnetism and light it is: their laws stay true in a mirror. But one force does not: the weak force, which makes a free neutron decay. So a decaying neutron can show which hand is meant. Three ways to see it, each in the decay of a neutron into a proton, an electron and a third, very light particle without charge, the antineutrino.

New: emitted-along, the average, over many decays, of the dot of the direction in which a particle leaves with the spin axis of the neutron it comes from; and spin-along-motion, the average dot of a particle's own spin axis with the direction in which it moves.

; 1. the electron leaves more often against the neutron's spin axis
; than along it
(observed (< (emitted-along electron neutron) 0))
(observed (> (emitted-along electron neutron) (neg 1)))
; the antineutrino does the opposite: it leaves more often along the
; spin axis
(observed (> (emitted-along antineutrino neutron) 0))

; 2. the electron itself spins against its motion, on average
(observed (< (spin-along-motion electron neutron) 0))
(observed (> (spin-along-motion electron neutron) (neg 1)))

; 3. the antineutrino spins along its motion: always, or so nearly
; that no difference has been seen
(observed (> (spin-along-motion antineutrino neutron) (− 1 (÷ 1 (^ 2 16)))))
(observed (≤ (spin-along-motion antineutrino neutron) 1))
; it has no charge, and much less mass than the electron
(observed (= (charge antineutrino) 0))
(observed (< (mass antineutrino) (mass electron)))

In a mirror, each of these would come out the other way. So a reader who has used the other hand for cross finds out by watching neutrons decay: with its hand, the electrons would leave more often along the spin axis, which this lesson says they do not. All three ways come from the weak force; nothing else in physics, as far as is known, tells left from right. (Living things on Earth also prefer one hand in their molecules, but that is a fact about us, not about nature.)
