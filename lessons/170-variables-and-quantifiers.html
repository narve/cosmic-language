variables and quantifiers

New: variables (u, v, w), the quantifiers ∀ ("for all") and ∃ ("there exists"), the domains ℕ (natural numbers) and ℚ (rational numbers), and implies.

A quantifier binds one variable to a domain: (∀ u ℕ …) means "for every natural number u, …". Every variable is bound in the sentence where it appears, and a variable in one sentence has nothing to do with the same variable in the next.

From instances to a law

The receiver has seen many instances already. The law states them all at once. From here on, a law often comes with a few of its instances, although the law alone says everything: the instances show how to read it.

(= (+ 0 0) 0) (= (+ 3 0) 3) (= (+ 7 0) 7) (= (+ 255 0) 255)
(∀ u ℕ (= (+ u 0) u))

More laws over ℕ

(= (× 4 1) 4) (= (× 9 1) 9) (= (× 255 1) 255)
(∀ u ℕ (= (× u 1) u))  (∀ u ℕ (= (× u 0) 0))  (∀ u ℕ (= (− u u) 0))
(∀ u ℕ (< u (+ u 1)))
nothing in ℕ is below 0
(∀ u ℕ (or (= u 0) (> u 0)))

Two variables

Each quantifier binds one variable, so two variables need two nested quantifiers.

(= (+ 2 5) (+ 5 2)) (= (+ 3 4) (+ 4 3)) (= (× 2 5) (× 5 2))
(∀ u ℕ (∀ v ℕ (= (+ u v) (+ v u))))
(∀ u ℕ (∀ v ℕ (= (× u v) (× v u))))
(∀ u ℕ (∀ v ℕ (∀ w ℕ (= (× u (+ v w)) (+ (× u v) (× u w))))))

The name does not matter

(∀ u ℕ (= (+ u 0) u))  (∀ v ℕ (= (+ v 0) v))  (∀ w ℕ (= (+ w 0) w))

There exists

Each example is followed by the number that makes it true.

(∃ u ℕ (= (+ u 2) 5))  (= (+ 3 2) 5)
(∃ u ℕ (= (× u u) 9))  (= (× 3 3) 9)
(∃ u ℕ (> u 255))      (> (big 1 0) 255)

ℚ: the rational numbers

The same laws hold over ℚ:

(∀ u ℚ (= (+ u 0) u))  (∀ u ℚ (∀ v ℚ (= (+ u v) (+ v u))))

But ℚ contains numbers that ℕ does not:

(∃ u ℚ (< u 0))           (∀ u ℕ (not (< u 0)))
a number between 0 and 1
(∃ u ℚ (< 0 u 1))         (∀ u ℕ (not (< 0 u 1)))
⅓
(∃ u ℚ (= (× u 3) 1))     (∀ u ℕ (≠ (× u 3) 1))
every number has an opposite
(∀ u ℚ (∃ v ℚ (= (+ u v) 0)))

implies

First its truth table, then its use: stating something only where a condition holds.

(= (implies true true) true)   (= (implies true false) false)
(= (implies false true) true)  (= (implies false false) true)
(∀ u ℚ (implies (< u 2) (< u 3)))
(∀ u ℕ (implies (> u 5) (> u 2)))
only where u is not 0
(∀ u ℚ (implies (≠ u 0) (= (× u (÷ 1 u)) 1)))

Inside a quantifier, a relation such as (< u 2) is an open statement: it holds for some values of u and not for others. This does not break the rule that nothing false is written, which applies to closed relations.
