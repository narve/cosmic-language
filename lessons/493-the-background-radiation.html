the background radiation

From every direction in the sky comes a faint, cold glow: the light left over from a time when the universe was hot and dense. New: background, the temperature of that glow in a given direction, as seen by an observer at rest relative to it. An observer who moves through it sees it warmer ahead and colder behind: for one moving as slowly as the Sun, by about a thousandth. The bound below, 1/2⁸, holds for such an observer too, so a reader need not know its own motion to check it.


; it is a temperature, and nearly the same in every direction: two
; directions differ by less than 1/2⁸ of it
(∀ u directions (= (dim (background u)) Θ))
(observed (∀ u directions (∀ v directions
  (< (abs (− (background u) (background v))) (× (÷ 1 (^ 2 8)) (background v))))))

; its average over the sky: between 163/2¹⁴ and 164/2¹⁴ of water's
; triple point
(observed (< (÷ 163 (^ 2 14)) (÷ (average v directions (background v)) (triple-point water)) (÷ 164 (^ 2 14))))

The small differences from direction to direction form a pattern that is the same for every observer in a very large region around us: the glow comes from so far away that moving even across a whole galaxy does not change it. So the pattern can serve as a reference for directions that any such reader can find, and finding it says nothing about where the reader is.

Its largest feature defines three axes. New: first-axis, second-axis and third-axis. The first is the direction along which the glow is, on the whole, most uneven: no other direction gives a larger average of (temperature − mean temperature) × ((dot with the direction)² − 1/3). The second does the same among the directions at right angles to the first, and the third is the cross of the first two.


; the first axis: no direction gives a larger value
(observed (∀ w directions
  (≤ (average u directions (× (− (background u) (average v directions (background v))) (− (^ (dot w u) 2) (÷ 1 3))))
     (average u directions (× (− (background u) (average v directions (background v))) (− (^ (dot first-axis u) 2) (÷ 1 3)))))))
; the second axis: the same, among the directions at right angles to
; the first
(observed (∀ w directions
  (implies (= (dot w first-axis) 0)
    (≤ (average u directions (× (− (background u) (average v directions (background v))) (− (^ (dot w u) 2) (÷ 1 3))))
       (average u directions (× (− (background u) (average v directions (background v))) (− (^ (dot second-axis u) 2) (÷ 1 3))))))))
; they are directions at right angles to each other
(= (dot first-axis first-axis) 1)
(= (dot second-axis second-axis) 1)

(= (dot first-axis second-axis) 0)

; the third axis is at right angles to both
(= third-axis (cross first-axis second-axis))
(= (dot third-axis third-axis) 1)
(= (dot third-axis first-axis) 0)
(= (dot third-axis second-axis) 0)

The warmer-ahead, colder-behind pattern of a moving observer adds almost nothing to these averages, since (dot with the direction)² is the same ahead and behind; so the axes too are nearly the same for a reader at rest and for one moving as slowly as the Sun.

Each axis is a line: the rule above cannot tell its two ends apart. The hand of cross, and with it the third axis, is fixed by the lesson on spin, through the decay of the neutron. What remains open are the ends of the first two axes: a direction given in these axes is known up to turning the frame half round one of them, until one direction that a reader can find in another way is given in them.
