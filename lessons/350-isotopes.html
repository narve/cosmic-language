isotopes

New: isotope and stable. Atoms of the same element can have different numbers of neutrons. (isotope hydrogen 1) is hydrogen with 1 neutron.

; hydrogen with 1 neutron still has 1 proton
(= (count proton (isotope hydrogen 1)) 1)  (= (count neutron (isotope hydrogen 1)) 1)
; helium with 2 neutrons still has 2 protons
(= (count proton (isotope helium 2)) 2)  (= (count neutron (isotope helium 2)) 2)

; in general: an isotope has the neutrons it is given …
(∀ u elements (∀ v ℕ (= (count neutron (isotope u v)) v)))
; … and the protons of its element
(∀ u elements (∀ v ℕ (= (count proton (isotope u v)) (count proton u))))

Some isotopes last for ever; others fall apart after a while. Here are some that last:

; seven isotopes that last: hydrogen with 0 or 1 neutron, helium with 1 or 2,
; carbon with 6 or 7, oxygen with 8
(observed (stable (isotope hydrogen 0) (isotope hydrogen 1)
                  (isotope helium 1) (isotope helium 2)
                  (isotope carbon 6) (isotope carbon 7) (isotope oxygen 8)))

; stable can also be said of one isotope, or of a few
(observed (stable (isotope helium 2)))
(observed (stable (isotope carbon 6) (isotope oxygen 8)))

; hydrogen with one neutron weighs almost twice as much as hydrogen without: between 2 − 1/2⁹ and 2 times
(observed (< (− 2 (÷ 1 (^ 2 9))) (÷ (mass (isotope hydrogen 1)) (mass (isotope hydrogen 0))) 2))

Sources: AME2020 (masses), NUBASE2020 (which isotopes are stable). Details in the specification, §8.4.
