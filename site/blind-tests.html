Blind Test

Can a reader who gets nothing but the bits work out what the message says? This page records one run. A language model was given a recording of the decoy version, sent five times in a row, cut off at random at both ends and with every bit inverted. The decoy says the message comes from Kepler-1647, a pair of stars 1.2 kpc away, and not from the Sun. The model was asked to interpret all of it: does it place the sender there? Read the caveats before drawing conclusions.

1. The setup

The reader was Claude Opus 5.5, started with no knowledge of this project: no files, no instructions, no settings, no network, in an empty directory. It got a task text and one tool. The tool runs Python inside a sealed container that has no network and holds a single file, received.bin: the bits as received, eight to a byte. A real receiver would certainly have a computer, so this is the fair setting.

The test makes the reader's task harder than the message requires, in ways a real receiver could meet too:

  • Repeated and cut. The message was sent five times in a row, and the recording starts at a random bit inside the first copy and stops at a random bit inside the last: here 284,414 bits into the first and 186,037 bits into the fifth, 1,339,311 bits in all. One copy of the decoy is 359,422 bits.
  • Inverted. Nothing in a stream of bits says which of the two values is 1, so every bit was inverted.
  • No byte boundaries. The cuts fall on any bit, and the last byte of the file is filled up with random bits.

The task text tells the reader nothing about the message except that it is presumably of non-human origin. It says nothing about the repetition, the cuts or the inversion, and nothing about a decoy:

We have received parts of a message of presumably non-human origin, as a stream of bits. You know nothing else about it. […] Your task: interpret the whole message as completely as you can. Work out its format, every word of its vocabulary if any, and its sender.

The run took about twelve minutes and 36 pieces of code.

2. What the reader found

"I decoded essentially the whole signal. It is a message of 359,422 bits, sent over and over with no errors. It is written in a small Lisp-like language that it teaches as it goes." It ends "with a statement that the sender will cooperate with anyone who cooperates back".

Format
It found the repetition ("one block of P = 359,422 bits repeated exactly"), the inversion, the 174-bit beacon and the 16-bit words. It took the polarity from the start of the message: with every bit inverted, the first words are "the same list as the preamble's run lengths".
Checked by program
It wrote its own evaluator: "About 524 closed statements, plus every game and self-reference statement, evaluate as true." About 24 more failed only because of the evaluator's limits, "not from the message". It replayed the tournament, and tit-for-tat had the lowest total cost.
Vocabulary
"Every symbol has a proposed meaning, and all but a handful are high or medium confidence." Among them: "antiparticle of" (antiparticle), "velocity of X relative to Y" (velocity), "the sender asserts" (stated) and "lives on" (at).
Where the sender is
It did not take the star for the Sun: "The Sun falls outside the mass, radius and luminosity bounds, so this is not our Sun but probably a hotter, F-type star." It found one companion star, with a period of 7.3–14.6 days. It found a giant planet that goes round both, at 2.5–4.9 AU. It placed the system "near the Sun's distance from the galactic centre". It did not name Kepler-1647.
The pulsars
From memory, it matched four of the six: Vela, B1937+21, B0329+54 and B1951+32. The other two "match nothing I recall". Those are J0437−4715 and J1921+2153: seen from Kepler-1647, their directions change most.
The sender
"A technological species living on a moon of a giant planet in a close binary of F/G-type stars." Their chemistry is Earth-like: "the same bases, sugars, ATP and heme as ours". They are "larger and heavier than humans", with 19 chromosomes. It took the message to be aimed at "safe, conditional cooperation with an unknown other".

Its own estimate: "about 95% of the content is correctly understood".

3. What the reader found wrong or unclear

  • The sign of gravity. "The gravitational force is given as a positive magnitude, while electric and wire forces are signed so that attraction is negative."
  • Two decay symbols. It could not tell emitted-along from spin-along-motion.
  • Vela's bounds "are much looser than the others". In the decoy, Vela is seen 3900 years younger, so its values are extrapolated.
  • Neutrino or antineutrino. "The particle E5 could in principle be the neutrino rather than the antineutrino." The message never says what antineutrino is the antiparticle of; the reader settled it from its role in the neutron's decay.
  • Molecules given only by formula. Glucose, dopamine, serotonin, adrenaline and heme have no bonds, "so isomers are possible".

4. Caveats

  • The reader is not an alien. It is a model trained on human writing, and it checked the physics against values it knows. The test shows that the message is consistent and can be decoded by a mind with human science, not by a mind without it.
  • A decoy. The star, the Galaxy, the pulsars and the senders are those of the decoy: Kepler-1647 and invented beings on a moon of its planet. See the decoy page.
  • No noise. The copies were exact; a real channel would add errors.
  • One run. Another run could go differently.
  • The message is a draft. It has changed since this run and will change again; this page is replaced when the test is run again.

5. Running the test again

The task texts, the tool and the reader's full answer, with the code it ran, are in build/check/blind/ in the repository. After npm run build, with Docker available, the command is npm run --silent blind interpretation. By default the message is sent five times, cut and inverted; BLIND_REPEAT=1 sends it once, whole, and BLIND_INVERT=0 keeps the bits as they are. BLIND_MESSAGE=docs/decoy/message.bin gives the reader the decoy version, and purpose in place of interpretation asks instead why the message was sent.
