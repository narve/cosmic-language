Blind Test

Can a reader who gets nothing but the bits work out what the message says? This page records one run. A language model was given a recording of the decoy version, sent five times in a row, cut off at random at both ends and with every bit inverted. The decoy says the message comes from Kepler-1647, a pair of stars 1.2 kpc away, and not from the Sun. The model was asked to interpret all of it: does it place the sender there? Read the caveats before drawing conclusions.

1. The setup

The reader was Claude Opus 5.5, started with no knowledge of this project: no files, no instructions, no settings, no network, in an empty directory. It got a task text and one tool. The tool runs Python inside a sealed container that has no network and holds a single file, received.bin: the bits as received, eight to a byte. A real receiver would certainly have a computer, so this is the fair setting.

The test makes the reader's task harder than the message requires, in ways a real receiver could meet too:

  • Repeated and cut. The message was sent five times in a row, and the recording starts at a random bit inside the first copy and stops at a random bit inside the last: here 180,088 bits into the first and 158,730 bits into the fifth, 1,421,322 bits in all. One copy of the decoy is 360,670 bits.
  • Inverted. Nothing in a stream of bits says which of the two values is 1, so every bit was inverted.
  • No byte boundaries. The cuts fall on any bit, and the last byte of the file is filled up with random bits.

The task text tells the reader nothing about the message except that it is presumably of non-human origin. It says nothing about the repetition, the cuts or the inversion, and nothing about a decoy:

We have received parts of a message of presumably non-human origin, as a stream of bits. You know nothing else about it. […] Your task: interpret the whole message as completely as you can. Work out its format, every word of its vocabulary if any, and its sender.

The run took about 17 minutes and 42 pieces of code.

2. What the reader found

"I decoded the whole message. It is a self-teaching, Lisp-like formal language of 1,226 statements." It ends "with a self-description of the sender: an intelligent species with its own genetic chemistry, living on a moon of a giant planet that orbits a binary star in a galaxy like ours". "Every statement I could check, by computation or against physics I know, came out true."

Format
It found the repetition ("one 360,670-bit message repeated"), the inversion ("the rare 0s carry the information"), the 174-bit preamble and the 16-bit tokens, where "the first byte is a type (only values 0–9 occur), the second a value". The message's own sentences about itself confirmed where it starts.
Checked by program
It wrote its own evaluator: "537 of the first 600 came out true. None was genuinely false"; the rest failed on its finite samples of infinite sets. It replayed the tournament: "My simulation reproduces every stated score."
Vocabulary
"Every symbol has a proposed meaning, and only a few are low confidence." Among them: "the sender asserts (unverifiable claims)" (stated), "lives on / inhabits" (at), "velocity of X relative to Y" (velocity) and "complementary base" (partner). Among the least certain: the two decay symbols, stable, and the difference between stated and observed.
Where the sender is
"It is not the Sun: too luminous and massive, and it is a binary." It found the companion star (period 7.3–14.6 days), the giant planet that goes round both (2.5–4.9 AU) and "a location at a Sun-like galactocentric distance". It did not name Kepler-1647.
The pulsars
From their periods, it matched four of the six from memory: Vela, J0437−4715, B1937+21 and B1919+21. "I cannot check the directions."
The sender
Its hereditary molecule is a "left-handed double helix", unlike DNA, with "four bases that are not ours, but are known chemical analogues of ours"; it named them from their formulas (2,6-diaminopurine, 5-hydroxymethyluracil, xanthine, 2,4-diaminopyrimidine) and the sugar as threose. "19 chromosomes, 8.6–17 billion base pairs in total". Body mass 132–265 kg, size 2.7–5.4 m, body temperature 290–307 K, "water-based carbon chemistry, warm-blooded range".

Its own estimate: "about 90% of the message's content".

3. What the reader found wrong or unclear

  • The game, the wrong way round. The game is told in losses: a smaller number is better. The reader read the numbers as gains, so it took cooperation for defection and the other way round. It still reproduced every score. But it read our closing strategy as one that "opens with v5 (in its own game, the dominant 'defect' move)", right "after proving that this strategy scores worst": "a warning, an honesty test, or a puzzle".
  • The decay symbols. emitted-along and spin-along-motion are its least certain readings ("low-med").
  • Directions it could not check. The directions in the frame of the background radiation "need CMB and pulsar sky maps"; the pulsars are matched by their periods only.
  • Coarse precision. "Most quantities are given only to within a factor of 2, so the location data narrows the search rather than pinning it down."
  • The moon. "The moon itself is never described."

4. Caveats

  • The reader is not an alien. It is a model trained on human writing, and it checked the physics against values it knows. The test shows that the message is consistent and can be decoded by a mind with human science, not by a mind without it.
  • A decoy. The star, the Galaxy, the pulsars and the senders are those of the decoy: Kepler-1647 and invented beings on a moon of its planet. See the decoy page.
  • No noise. The copies were exact; a real channel would add errors.
  • One run. Another run could go differently.
  • The message is a draft. It has changed since this run and will change again; this page is replaced when the test is run again.

5. Running the test again

The task texts, the tool and the reader's full answer, with the code it ran, are in build/check/blind/ in the repository. After npm run build, with Docker available, the command is npm run --silent blind interpretation. By default the message is sent five times, cut and inverted; BLIND_REPEAT=1 sends it once, whole, and BLIND_INVERT=0 keeps the bits as they are. BLIND_MESSAGE=docs/decoy/message.bin gives the reader the decoy version, and purpose in place of interpretation asks instead why the message was sent.
