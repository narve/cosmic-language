division and fractions

Division follows the same pattern as subtraction. First, only divisions that come out even:


; dividing by 2
(= (÷ 2 2) 1)
(= (÷ 4 2) 2)
(= (÷ 6 2) 3)
(= (÷ 8 2) 4)

; other divisions; a number other than 0 divided by itself is 1,
; 0 divided by any other number is 0, dividing by 1 changes nothing
(= (÷ 6 3) 2)
(= (÷ 8 2) 4)
(= (÷ 9 9) 1)
(= (÷ 0 5) 0)
(= (÷ 7 1) 7)

; ÷ undoes ×: (6 ÷ 3) × 3 = 6
(= (× (÷ 6 3) 3) 6)

Then divisions that do not come out even. No new symbol is needed: the result is simply written as a division, reduced to lowest terms.


; a half: two halves make 1, and a half lies between 0 and 1
(= (+ (÷ 1 2) (÷ 1 2)) 1)
(= (× (÷ 1 2) 2) 1)
(< 0 (÷ 1 2) 1)

; a third: three thirds make 1
(= (× (÷ 1 3) 3) 1)
(= (+ (÷ 1 3) (÷ 1 3) (÷ 1 3)) 1)

; the same value, in lowest terms: 2/6 = 1/3 and 4/6 = 2/3
(= (÷ 2 6) (÷ 1 3))
(= (÷ 4 6) (÷ 2 3))

; adding fractions: 1/2 + 1/3 = 5/6
(= (+ (÷ 1 2) (÷ 1 3)) (÷ 5 6))
; fractions lie between whole numbers: 0, 1/3, 1/2, 1
(< 0 (÷ 1 3) (÷ 1 2) 1)

; with negative numbers: the neg can be written in front
(= (÷ (neg 6) 3) (neg 2))
(= (÷ 1 (neg 3)) (neg (÷ 1 3)))

With ÷, the message has fractions, and with them all the rational numbers: every one can be written as (÷ p q) in lowest terms, with neg in front if it is negative. Fractions are exact, which suits a message in which every statement is true. Decimals and floating-point numbers, which round, will probably never be needed: where a value is measured rather than exact, the message gives a range between two fractions instead.

Division by zero never appears in the message.
