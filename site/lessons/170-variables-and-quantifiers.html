variables and quantifiers

New: variables (u, v, w, z), the quantifiers ∀ ("for all") and ∃ ("there exists"), the domains ℕ (natural numbers) and ℚ (rational numbers), and implies.

A quantifier binds one variable to the values it runs through: (∀ u (list 1 2 3) …) means "for every u among 1, 2 and 3, …". Every variable is bound in the sentence where it appears, and a variable in one sentence has nothing to do with the same variable in the next.

For all, over a list

The lesson starts with short lists, where everything can be checked by hand. A sentence with ∀ says the same as all its instances together: it is the and of them.


; three instances: 1, 2 and 3 are each less than 4
(< 1 4)
(< 2 4)
(< 3 4)

; the same in one sentence: every u in the list is less than 4
(∀ u (list 1 2 3) (< u 4))
; u stands for each item in turn: it is 1, or 2, or 3
(∀ u (list 1 2 3) (or (= u 1) (= u 2) (= u 3)))
; what it means: put each item in the place of u, and join the three
; results with and
(= (∀ u (list 1 2 3) (< u 4))
   (and (< 1 4) (< 2 4) (< 3 4)))

; more lists, more statements
(∀ u (list 5 7 9) (> u 4))
(∀ u (list 0 3 7) (= (+ u 0) u))
(∀ u (list 2 4 6) (≠ u 5))

; a list of one item: just that one instance
(= (∀ u (list 5) (> u 4)) (> 5 4))

There exists, over a list

∃ says that at least one item makes the statement true. Each example is followed by an item that does.


; one of the items is 2
(∃ u (list 1 2 3) (= u 2))
(= 2 2)

; one of the items is greater than 2: it is 3
(∃ u (list 1 2 3) (> u 2))
(> 3 2)

; each of the three items is a value u can take
(∃ u (list 1 2 3) (= u 1))
(∃ u (list 1 2 3) (= u 3))

; "some item is 2" is true, "every item is 2" is false
(= (∃ u (list 1 2 3) (= u 2)) true)
(= (∀ u (list 1 2 3) (= u 2)) false)

; what holds for every item holds for some item
(∀ u (list 1 2 3) (< u 4))
(∃ u (list 1 2 3) (< u 4))

; some item is 2: the same as "not every item differs from 2"
(= (∃ u (list 1 2 3) (= u 2)) (not (∀ u (list 1 2 3) (≠ u 2))))

From a list to ℕ

A list ends. The natural numbers do not: ℕ stands for 0, 1, 2, 3 and so on without end. A law over ℕ cannot be checked instance by instance, but it reads the same way. From here on, a law often comes with a few of its instances, although the law alone says everything: the instances show how to read it.


; four instances: adding 0 changes nothing
(= (+ 0 0) 0)
(= (+ 3 0) 3)
(= (+ 7 0) 7)
(= (+ 255 0) 255)

; the same over a list of those four numbers
(∀ u (list 0 3 7 255) (= (+ u 0) u))
; the law: for every natural number u, u + 0 = u
(∀ u ℕ (= (+ u 0) u))

; ℕ starts at 0 …
(∃ u ℕ (= u 0))
; … and never ends: every number has a next one
(∀ u ℕ (∃ v ℕ (= v (+ u 1))))

More laws over ℕ


; three instances, then the law: times 1 changes nothing
(= (× 4 1) 4)
(= (× 9 1) 9)
(= (× 255 1) 255)

; also: times 0 gives 0, and a number minus itself is 0
(∀ u ℕ (= (× u 1) u))
(∀ u ℕ (= (× u 0) 0))
(∀ u ℕ (= (− u u) 0))

; every number is less than the next
(∀ u ℕ (< u (+ u 1)))
; nothing in ℕ is below 0
(∀ u ℕ (or (= u 0) (> u 0)))

Two variables

Each quantifier binds one variable, so two variables need two nested quantifiers.


; three instances: the order does not matter
(= (+ 2 5) (+ 5 2))
(= (+ 3 4) (+ 4 3))
(= (× 2 5) (× 5 2))

; the laws: u + v = v + u and u × v = v × u
(∀ u ℕ (∀ v ℕ (= (+ u v) (+ v u))))
(∀ u ℕ (∀ v ℕ (= (× u v) (× v u))))

; three variables: u × (v + w) = u × v + u × w
(∀ u ℕ (∀ v ℕ (∀ w ℕ (= (× u (+ v w)) (+ (× u v) (× u w))))))
; and (u + v) + w = u + (v + w)
(∀ u ℕ (∀ v ℕ (∀ w ℕ (= (+ (+ u v) w) (+ u (+ v w))))))

; four variables: the order of a sum does not matter
(∀ u ℕ (∀ v ℕ (∀ w ℕ (∀ z ℕ (= (+ u v w z) (+ z w v u))))))
; and (u + v) × (w + z) = u × w + u × z + v × w + v × z
(∀ u ℕ (∀ v ℕ (∀ w ℕ (∀ z ℕ
  (= (× (+ u v) (+ w z)) (+ (× u w) (× u z) (× v w) (× v z)))))))

Laws about lists

With variables, what the lesson on lists showed by examples can be stated in general:


; a list of two items has length 2, and its first item is the first
; one given
(∀ u ℕ (∀ v ℕ (= (length (list u v)) 2)))
(∀ u ℕ (∀ v ℕ (= (item (list u v) 1) u)))

; the last item is the one at the position of the length
(∀ u ℕ (∀ v ℕ (= (item (list u v) (length (list u v))) v)))

The name does not matter


; the same law four times, with four different variables
(∀ u ℕ (= (+ u 0) u))
(∀ v ℕ (= (+ v 0) v))
(∀ w ℕ (= (+ w 0) w))
(∀ z ℕ (= (+ z 0) z))

There exists, in ℕ

Each example is followed by the number that makes it true.


; there is a number that gives 5 when 2 is added: it is 3
(∃ u ℕ (= (+ u 2) 5))
(= (+ 3 2) 5)

; there is a number whose square is 9: it is 3
(∃ u ℕ (= (× u u) 9))
(= (× 3 3) 9)

; there is a number above 255: 256 is one
(∃ u ℕ (> u 255))
(> (big 1 0) 255)

ℚ: the rational numbers

ℚ holds every quotient of two natural numbers, and the opposite of each. So it contains the natural numbers, the fractions between them, and the negative numbers:


; 1/2, 2/3 and neg 3 are in ℚ
(∃ u ℚ (= u (÷ 1 2)))
(∃ u ℚ (= u (÷ 2 3)))
(∃ u ℚ (= u (neg 3)))

; every natural number is in ℚ
(∀ u ℕ (∃ v ℚ (= u v)))

; every quotient of two natural numbers is in ℚ (v + 1 is never 0)
(∀ u ℕ (∀ v ℕ (∃ w ℚ (= w (÷ u (+ v 1))))))
; and nothing else is: every number in ℚ is such a quotient, or the
; neg of one
(∀ w ℚ (∃ u ℕ (∃ v ℕ
  (or (= w (÷ u (+ v 1))) (= w (neg (÷ u (+ v 1))))))))

The same laws hold over ℚ:


; adding 0 changes nothing, and the order of a sum does not matter
(∀ u ℚ (= (+ u 0) u))
(∀ u ℚ (∀ v ℚ (= (+ u v) (+ v u))))

But ℚ contains numbers that ℕ does not:


; ℚ has a number below 0; ℕ has none
(∃ u ℚ (< u 0))
(∀ u ℕ (not (< u 0)))

; ℚ has a number between 0 and 1; ℕ has none
(∃ u ℚ (< 0 u 1))
(∀ u ℕ (not (< 0 u 1)))

; ℚ has a number that gives 1 when multiplied by 3 (it is ⅓); ℕ has
; none
(∃ u ℚ (= (× u 3) 1))
(∀ u ℕ (≠ (× u 3) 1))

; every number in ℚ has an opposite: the two add up to 0
(∀ u ℚ (∃ v ℚ (= (+ u v) 0)))

implies

First its truth table, then its use: stating something only where a condition holds.


; the truth table: implies is false only when the first is true and
; the second false
(= (implies true true) true)
(= (implies true false) false)

(= (implies false true) true)
(= (implies false false) true)

; whatever is below 2 is below 3
(∀ u ℚ (implies (< u 2) (< u 3)))
; whatever is above 5 is above 2
(∀ u ℕ (implies (> u 5) (> u 2)))
; only where u is not 0: u × (1 ÷ u) = 1
(∀ u ℚ (implies (≠ u 0) (= (× u (÷ 1 u)) 1)))

Inside a quantifier, a relation such as (< u 2) is an open statement: it holds for some values of u and not for others. This does not break the rule that nothing false is written, which applies to closed relations.
