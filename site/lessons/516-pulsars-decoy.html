The light from a pulsar also reaches Kepler-1647 earlier or later than it reaches the Sun, by up to 4000 years, so a pulsar is seen at another age: Vela turns faster. And the light of the explosion that made the Crab pulsar has not reached Kepler-1647 yet, so there the name crab-pulsar stands for another young pulsar in the remnant of an exploded star, PSR B1951+32 in Cygnus. Some stars end as neutron stars: a ball of neutrons the width of a city, turning anywhere from about once a second to hundreds of times a second, and sending out a beam of radio light that sweeps past our star once each turn. These pulsars keep time better than almost anything, and each has its own period, so they serve as landmarks. New: the domain pulsars, pulse-period (the time of one turn), slowing (how much longer each turn gets per time: a plain number, time per time), and six pulsars by name. ; a pulsar is a body; its period is a time, and the period grows
; a little all the time
(∀ u pulsars (∃ v bodies (= u v)))
(∀ u pulsars (= (dim (pulse-period u)) T))
(observed (∀ u pulsars (> (slowing u) 0)))

Each pulsar is given by its period, by how fast it slows, and by its direction from our star in the three directions of the Galaxy. Seen from anywhere else, the same pulsars lie in other directions; from the directions of several of them, a reader can find the place they were seen from. The periods are given only to within a factor of 2: enough to tell which pulsars are meant, but not how long ago the message was sent.
