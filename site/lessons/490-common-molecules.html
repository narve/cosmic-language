common molecules

Atoms other than the noble gases join into molecules. count tells how many atoms of an element a molecule contains, and counting goes on through the atoms to their particles:


; hydrogen gas, the simplest molecule: two hydrogen atoms
(= (count hydrogen dihydrogen) 2)
; counting through the atoms: 2 protons and 2 electrons
(= (count proton dihydrogen) 2)
(= (count electron dihydrogen) 2)

; water: two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom
(= (count hydrogen water) 2)
(= (count oxygen water) 1)

; counting through the atoms: 1 + 1 + 8 = 10 protons, and as many
; electrons
(= (count proton water) 10)
(= (count electron water) 10)

; methane: one carbon and four hydrogen
(= (count carbon methane) 1)
(= (count hydrogen methane) 4)

; nitrogen has 7 protons, between carbon and oxygen
(= (count proton nitrogen) 7)
(< (count proton carbon) (count proton nitrogen) (count proton oxygen))

; ammonia: one nitrogen and three hydrogen
(= (count nitrogen ammonia) 1)
(= (count hydrogen ammonia) 3)

; carbon dioxide: one carbon and two oxygen
(= (count carbon carbon-dioxide) 1)
(= (count oxygen carbon-dioxide) 2)

atoms gives a molecule's atoms as a list. bonds gives its bonds: each bond is a list of the positions of two atoms and the number of electron pairs they share.


; hydrogen gas: its two atoms share one pair
(= (atoms dihydrogen) (list hydrogen hydrogen))
(= (bonds dihydrogen) (list (list 1 2 1)))

; water: the oxygen (atom 1) shares one pair with each hydrogen
; (atoms 2 and 3)
(= (atoms water) (list oxygen hydrogen hydrogen))
(= (bonds water) (list (list 1 2 1) (list 1 3 1)))

; carbon dioxide: the carbon shares two pairs with each oxygen
(= (atoms carbon-dioxide) (list carbon oxygen oxygen))
(= (bonds carbon-dioxide) (list (list 1 2 2) (list 1 3 2)))

; methane: the carbon shares one pair with each of the four
; hydrogens
(= (atoms methane) (list carbon hydrogen hydrogen hydrogen hydrogen))
(= (bonds methane) (list (list 1 2 1) (list 1 3 1) (list 1 4 1) (list 1 5 1)))

; ammonia: the nitrogen shares one pair with each of the three
; hydrogens
(= (atoms ammonia) (list nitrogen hydrogen hydrogen hydrogen))
(= (bonds ammonia) (list (list 1 2 1) (list 1 3 1) (list 1 4 1)))

; the count of an element in a molecule is its count in the list of
; atoms
(= (count hydrogen water) (count hydrogen (atoms water)))

; a molecule is a token of a new type, molecule: 9
(= (type (quote water)) molecule)

All the molecules so far are made of hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen and oxygen, the most common elements that react. So are most of the molecules found between the stars, in the clouds where stars form. Here are six more of them, each given by its atoms and its bonds. Carbon monoxide is the most common molecule there after hydrogen gas. The others show that carbon can share two or three pairs with one neighbour, and can join other carbon atoms into chains.


; carbon monoxide: one carbon and one oxygen, sharing three pairs
(= (count carbon carbon-monoxide) 1)
(= (count oxygen carbon-monoxide) 1)
(= (atoms carbon-monoxide) (list carbon oxygen))
(= (bonds carbon-monoxide) (list (list 1 2 3)))

; hydrogen cyanide: hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen; the carbon shares
; one pair with the hydrogen and three with the nitrogen
(= (count hydrogen hydrogen-cyanide) 1)
(= (count carbon hydrogen-cyanide) 1)
(= (count nitrogen hydrogen-cyanide) 1)
(= (atoms hydrogen-cyanide) (list hydrogen carbon nitrogen))
(= (bonds hydrogen-cyanide) (list (list 1 2 1) (list 2 3 3)))

; formaldehyde: the carbon shares two pairs with the oxygen and one
; with each of two hydrogens
(= (count carbon formaldehyde) 1)
(= (count hydrogen formaldehyde) 2)
(= (count oxygen formaldehyde) 1)
(= (atoms formaldehyde) (list carbon oxygen hydrogen hydrogen))
(= (bonds formaldehyde) (list (list 1 2 2) (list 1 3 1) (list 1 4 1)))

; methanol: three hydrogens on the carbon, and one on the oxygen
(= (count carbon methanol) 1)
(= (count hydrogen methanol) 4)
(= (count oxygen methanol) 1)
(= (atoms methanol) (list carbon oxygen hydrogen hydrogen hydrogen hydrogen))
(= (bonds methanol) (list (list 1 2 1) (list 1 3 1) (list 1 4 1) (list 1 5 1) (list 2 6 1)))

; acetylene: two carbons sharing three pairs, a hydrogen on each
(= (count carbon acetylene) 2)
(= (count hydrogen acetylene) 2)
(= (atoms acetylene) (list carbon carbon hydrogen hydrogen))
(= (bonds acetylene) (list (list 1 2 3) (list 1 3 1) (list 2 4 1)))

; cyanoacetylene: a chain of hydrogen, three carbons and nitrogen
(= (count hydrogen cyanoacetylene) 1)
(= (count carbon cyanoacetylene) 3)
(= (count nitrogen cyanoacetylene) 1)
(= (atoms cyanoacetylene) (list hydrogen carbon carbon carbon nitrogen))
(= (bonds cyanoacetylene) (list (list 1 2 1) (list 2 3 3) (list 3 4 1) (list 4 5 3)))

; counting through the atoms: carbon monoxide has 6 + 8 = 14
; electrons
(= (count electron carbon-monoxide) 14)

Heavier elements join in too. Silicon and sulfur are among the most common elements after those four, and molecules with them are found between the stars as well, as are hydrogen chloride and, around old stars, sodium chloride.


; silicon monoxide: one silicon and one oxygen
(= (count silicon silicon-monoxide) 1)
(= (count oxygen silicon-monoxide) 1)
; 14 + 8 = 22 electrons
(= (count electron silicon-monoxide) 22)

; carbon monosulfide: one carbon and one sulfur
(= (count carbon carbon-monosulfide) 1)
(= (count sulfur carbon-monosulfide) 1)
(= (atoms carbon-monosulfide) (list carbon sulfur))

; hydrogen sulfide: built like water, with sulfur in place of oxygen
(= (count sulfur hydrogen-sulfide) 1)
(= (count hydrogen hydrogen-sulfide) 2)
(= (atoms hydrogen-sulfide) (list sulfur hydrogen hydrogen))
(= (bonds hydrogen-sulfide) (list (list 1 2 1) (list 1 3 1)))

; sulfur dioxide: one sulfur and two oxygen
(= (count sulfur sulfur-dioxide) 1)
(= (count oxygen sulfur-dioxide) 2)
(= (atoms sulfur-dioxide) (list sulfur oxygen oxygen))

; hydrogen chloride: one hydrogen and one chlorine, sharing one pair
(= (count hydrogen hydrogen-chloride) 1)
(= (count chlorine hydrogen-chloride) 1)
(= (atoms hydrogen-chloride) (list hydrogen chlorine))
(= (bonds hydrogen-chloride) (list (list 1 2 1)))

; sodium chloride: one sodium and one chlorine, held together by
; charge, not by a shared pair, so it has no bonds
(= (count sodium sodium-chloride) 1)
(= (count chlorine sodium-chloride) 1)
(= (atoms sodium-chloride) (list sodium chlorine))

At one particular temperature and pressure, a substance exists as solid, liquid and gas together: its triple point. Melting and boiling happen at different temperatures under different pressures, but the three forms exist together at one temperature and one pressure only, and both are fixed by the substance. So a triple point needs no pressure to be named with it, and it is the same everywhere. Here are four, from hydrogen gas to water. The first three are given as a part of water's: a ratio of two temperatures is a plain number and needs no unit.


; hydrogen gas, neon, argon and water, in the order of their triple
; points
(observed (< (triple-point dihydrogen) (triple-point neon) (triple-point argon) (triple-point water)))
; all four are temperatures
(= (dim (triple-point dihydrogen)) Θ)
(= (dim (triple-point neon)) Θ)

(= (dim (triple-point argon)) Θ)
(= (dim (triple-point water)) Θ)

; hydrogen's triple point is between 103/2¹¹ and 105/2¹¹ of water's:
; about 0.0505 (13.80 K) for hydrogen whose two spin forms are in
; balance, about 0.0511 (13.96 K) for the gas as it comes; the range
; holds for both
(observed (< (÷ 103 (^ 2 11))
              (÷ (triple-point dihydrogen) (triple-point water))
              (÷ 105 (^ 2 11))))
; neon's is between 736/2¹³ and 737/2¹³ of water's: about 0.0899
(observed (< (÷ (big 2 224) (^ 2 13))
              (÷ (triple-point neon) (triple-point water))
              (÷ (big 2 225) (^ 2 13))))
; argon's is between 1256/2¹² and 1257/2¹² of water's: about 0.3068
(observed (< (÷ (big 4 232) (^ 2 12))
              (÷ (triple-point argon) (triple-point water))
              (÷ (big 4 233) (^ 2 12))))

Water's own triple point is tied to quantities the message already has. Its thermal energy is compared with hydrogen's step from level 1 to level 2, and it is given in Planck temperatures. The ranges are narrow on purpose, about one part in 300 (roughly a kelvin wide): a wide range would also fit water's boiling, or any other temperature in between.


; kB × the triple point of water, divided by hydrogen's step from
; level 1 to level 2:
; between 302/2¹⁷ and 303/2¹⁷, which pins the temperature down to
; one part in 300
; (302 is written (big 1 46), because 256 + 46 = 302)
(observed (< (÷ (big 1 46) (^ 2 17))
              (÷ (× kB (triple-point water)) (− (level hydrogen 2) (level hydrogen 1)))
              (÷ (big 1 47) (^ 2 17))))

; in Planck units: between 156/2¹⁰⁶ and 157/2¹⁰⁶ Planck temperatures
(observed (< (÷ 156 (^ 2 106))
              (÷ (triple-point water) planck-temperature)
              (÷ 157 (^ 2 106))))

New: triple-pressure, the pressure at a triple point. A pressure is a force per area: mass ÷ (length × time²). The Planck units give a unit for it, the Planck mass divided by the Planck length and the square of the Planck time, so the triple points also give the message fixed pressures that are the same everywhere. Water's is the lowest of the four; it is given in that unit, and the others as many times water's.


; the four triple points in the order of their pressures: water has
; the lowest
(observed (< (triple-pressure water) (triple-pressure dihydrogen) (triple-pressure neon) (triple-pressure argon)))
; a pressure is a force per area: mass ÷ (length × time²)
(= (dim (triple-pressure water)) (÷ M (× L (^ T 2))))
(= (dim (÷ planck-mass (× planck-length (^ planck-time 2)))) (÷ M (× L (^ T 2))))

; water's triple-point pressure in the Planck unit of pressure:
; between 203/2³⁷⁶ and 204/2³⁷⁶
; (376 is written (big 1 120), because 256 + 120 = 376)
(observed (< (÷ 203 (^ 2 (big 1 120)))
              (÷ (triple-pressure water) (÷ planck-mass (× planck-length (^ planck-time 2))))
              (÷ 204 (^ 2 (big 1 120)))))

; hydrogen gas: between 11 and 12 times water's, about 11.5
(observed (< 11 (÷ (triple-pressure dihydrogen) (triple-pressure water)) 12))
; neon: between 141/2 and 143/2 times water's, about 70.9
(observed (< (÷ 141 2) (÷ (triple-pressure neon) (triple-pressure water)) (÷ 143 2)))
; argon: between 112 and 113 times water's
(observed (< 112 (÷ (triple-pressure argon) (triple-pressure water)) 113))

Sources: BIPM (water's triple point; the triple points and pressures of hydrogen, neon and argon), IAPWS G5-01 (water's triple-point pressure), NIST Chemistry WebBook (cross-check), CODATA 2022. Details in the specification, §8.4.
