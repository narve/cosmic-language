Chance was introduced as a proportion of a list. Nature has chances of its own: an unstable atom or particle decays at a moment that nothing can predict.

New: the domain durations and the functions lifetime, survival (the chance that something has not yet decayed after a given duration) and half-life. The example is the free neutron: on its own, it turns into a proton, an electron and one more very light particle without charge. 


; a neutron turns into a proton and an electron: the charge before
; equals the charge after
(observed (= (charge neutron) (+ (charge proton) (charge electron))))
; the neutron is heavier than the proton and the electron together,
; so it has mass to spare
(observed (> (mass neutron) (+ (mass proton) (mass electron))))

; its mean lifetime: about 880 seconds, or about 1.6 × 10⁴⁶ Planck
; times (between 2¹⁵³ and 2¹⁵⁴)
(observed (< (^ 2 153) (÷ (lifetime neutron) planck-time) (^ 2 154)))
; a lifetime is a time, and so is every duration
(= (dim (lifetime neutron)) T)
(∀ u durations (= (dim u) T))

The half-life is the duration after which the chance of survival is one half. That is exactly the chance of true when picking from true and false: 


; after one half-life, the chance that the neutron is still
; there is 1/2
(= (survival neutron (half-life neutron)) (÷ 1 2))
; 1/2 is also the chance of true when picking from true and false
(= (÷ 1 2) (chance true (list true false)))

A neutron has no memory: its chance of decaying does not depend on how long it has existed. So each half-life is a new, independent pick, and the chances multiply: 


; no memory: surviving u and then v is the same as surviving u + v,
; and the two chances multiply
(observed (∀ u durations (∀ v durations
  (= (survival neutron (+ u v))
     (× (survival neutron u) (survival neutron v))))))

; surviving u half-lives is like picking true u times in a row: the
; chance is 1/2 to the power u
(observed (∀ u ℕ
  (= (survival neutron (× u (half-life neutron)))
     (^ (chance true (list true false)) u))))

The first law is the product rule that defined exp, so the chance for any duration is exp of that duration times a fixed factor. The mean lifetime sets the factor: it is −1 ÷ the lifetime, so that after one lifetime the chance is 1/e: 


; the chance of surviving a duration u is exp(−u ÷ lifetime)
(observed (∀ u durations
  (= (survival neutron u)
     (exp (neg (÷ u (lifetime neutron)))))))

These laws give the chance for a single neutron. In a real sample of many neutrons, the part that survives is close to this chance, but the message makes no claim about any particular sample.

Unlike the stable isotopes, some isotopes decay in the same way: hydrogen with two neutrons, and carbon with eight. Carbon's half-life is between 256 and 512 times that of hydrogen: 


; the half-life of carbon with 8 neutrons, divided by that of
; hydrogen with 2 neutrons: between 2⁸ and 2⁹
(observed (< (^ 2 8)
              (÷ (half-life (isotope carbon 8)) (half-life (isotope hydrogen 2)))
              (^ 2 9)))
