Research survey

Previous Work

Earlier projects on messages that explain themselves, and what we can learn from them. Compiled from web searches and background knowledge. Anything not confirmed by search or fetch is marked [unverified]. See also the specification.

A. Mathematical and logical "languages" (learned from examples)

A1. Lincos (Lingua Cosmica)

  • 1960, Hans Freudenthal. Lincos: Design of a Language for Cosmic Intercourse, Part I (North-Holland).
  • Wikipedia: Lincos language
  • Summary: Lincos teaches natural numbers as runs of pulses separated by pauses, then introduces > < = + − through worked examples, with longer pauses marking that a new symbol is starting. From there it builds multiplication, division, variables, propositional logic, set theory, first-order logic and time ("Sec"). Chapter 3 stages dialogues between the characters "Ha" and "Hb", which teach saying (Inq), questions (marked with ?x), approval (Ben/Mal), truth (Ver/Fal), quoting and wanting; chapter 4 covers space, motion and mass. The planned Part II on matter, Earth and life was never written.
  • Relevance: The direct ancestor of our counting → relations → arithmetic → logic → quantifiers → definitions sequence. Borrow "teach by example, then pose an unsolved expression as a question". Use the Ha/Hb dialogues as a model for our communication layer. Avoid: Freudenthal moved quickly into concepts with weak grounding, and the physics half was never written. Criticism: never tested on naive readers.

A2. Astraglossa

  • 1952, Lancelot Hogben. A lecture, reprinted in Science in Authority (1963). Described at Wikipedia: Communication with extraterrestrial intelligence
  • Summary: Numbers are sent as unary "dashes" and operators as "flashes" (radioglyphs). Astronomical facts serve as shared reference points.
  • Relevance: The earliest separation of "operator token versus numeral". Our type chunks do the same thing more cleanly.

A3. Astrolinguistics / second-generation Lincos

  • 2013, Alexander Ollongren. Astrolinguistics: Design of a Linguistic System for Interstellar Communication Based on Logic (Springer, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4614-5468-7).
  • Wikipedia: Astrolinguistics; WorldCat
  • Summary: Lincos rebuilt on constructive logic (lambda calculus, Calculus of Constructions). Every expression is meant to be machine-verifiable.
  • Relevance: Matches our truth-checking validator closely. Consider a simple type discipline for DEF/λ. Avoid: the Calculus of Constructions is too heavy for a cold-start reader. There is no bit-level encoding, no error tolerance and no decodability test.

A4. DeVito–Oehrle language

  • 1990, Carl L. DeVito and Richard T. Oehrle. "A Language Based on the Fundamental Facts of Science", JBIS 43. Expanded in DeVito, Science, SETI, and Mathematics (2014).
  • University of Arizona: Carl DeVito; NBC News: how we might talk to space aliens
  • Summary: Syntax in the style of Lincos, but the vocabulary is anchored in chemistry and physics: elements, the periodic table and states of matter. From these it builds units of mass, energy, temperature and pressure. [unverified detail: units come from atom counts and the melting/boiling of water.]
  • Relevance: The strongest precedent for our units and chemistry layers. Combine it with the hydrogen line. Avoid: assuming Euclidean geometry or calculus without saying so.

A5. Busch's general-purpose binary language

  • 2009–2010, Michael W. Busch and Rachel M. Reddick. "Testing SETI Message Designs", arXiv 0911.3976.
  • arXiv 0911.3976
  • Summary: A 75-kilobit binary message covering mathematics and physics. A blind decode of a copy with the first 10–20 % and about 2 % of the later words missing succeeded in about 12 hours. The language was used in Lone Signal's hailing message (2013).
  • Relevance: A precedent for our blind test, including deliberately damaging the message. Weakness: N = 1, and the decoder was a scientist from the same culture. Our LLM test shares this weakness.

A6. CosmicOS (very relevant)

  • 2005– , Paul Fitzpatrick (MIT CSAIL).
  • GitHub: paulfitz/cosmicos; CosmicOS message (MIT CSAIL)
  • Summary: The message is a program in "Fritz", a stripped-down Lisp. The minimal alphabet is 0 1 ( ). Chapters are written in several languages and compiled to Fritz. It bootstraps from numbers and logic to running programs, logic-circuit rules and a simulated world. The whole message is evaluated by a test suite.
  • Relevance: Borrow the executable, checked build pipeline, the self-reference/self-evaluation, and the idea of an interpreter definition inside the message. Parentheses versus Polish notation is a choice we must make deliberately. Avoid: there is no bit-level framing, no beacon and no error-correcting code. Criticism: no published decodability test.

A7. McConnell: ACETI, "Beyond Contact", Logic Gate Matrices

  • Brian McConnell. Beyond Contact (O'Reilly, 2001), "Algorithmic Communication with ETI" (IAU, ~2004), The Alien Communication Handbook (Springer, 2021).
  • Centauri Dreams: alien computer programs; Springer
  • Summary: A primer in a "solve for x" style defines a VM or interpreted language, and the rest is programs. He stresses that FEC-coded or compressed data looks like noise, so he proposes an uncoded primer followed by an "unpack" function written in the taught language. He recommends Reed–Solomon over repetition.
  • Relevance: The sequence uncoded primer → language → decoder written in the language → strongly coded payload fits our "describes itself" goal. Avoid: never code the bootstrap layer with something that hasn't been taught yet.

A8. Minsky: "Communication with Alien Intelligence"

  • 1985, Marvin Minsky. Minsky: Communication with Alien Intelligence (MIT)
  • Summary: The economics principle (any intelligence develops objects, causes and goals) and the sparseness principle (arithmetic is an "island" every intelligence will find). Small Turing machines all ended up "counting".
  • Relevance: The theoretical basis for putting arithmetic first. Suggests description length as a metric for each new concept. Cautions that "intuitive" human concepts can hide complex machinery.

A9. Vakoch / METI International

  • Douglas Vakoch (ed.), Archaeology, Anthropology, and Interstellar Communication (NASA SP-2013-4413).
  • NASA: Archaeology, Anthropology, and Interstellar Communication (PDF)
  • Relevance: Compares the task to archaeological decipherment (Linear B, Maya). Useful for the "humans" layer.

B. Pictorial and physical messages (1970s)

B1. Drake 1961 and the Arecibo message (1974)

  • Scientific American: can you decode an alien message?; Scientific American: the Arecibo message turns 50
  • Summary: 1,679 bits = 23 × 73. Contains the numbers 1–10, the atomic numbers of H, C, N, O and P, nucleotides, DNA, a human figure, the Solar System and the telescope. Most of the experts who tried Drake's 1961 prototype (551 = 19 × 29) failed to decode it.
  • Relevance: Borrow the semiprime dimensions: Zenil and others found the width 23 with about 1.5 % of the bits wrong, and with far more only after each bit was repeated as a 6 × 6 block, so the robustness comes from redundancy. Avoid: idiosyncratic numerals, dense biochemistry, no redundancy. Note: it is in LLMs' training data, so the LLM test must avoid Arecibo-like surface features.

B2. Pioneer plaques (1972/73)

  • Wikipedia: Pioneer plaque
  • Summary: The hydrogen hyperfine transition defines units of length (about 21 cm) and time (about 0.7 ns). A pulsar map with 14 pulsars plus the galactic centre, with periods in hydrogen units.
  • Relevance: Borrow the hydrogen unit and the overdetermined pulsar map. Avoid: mixed notation (I/− for binary), precision theatre (more digits than are actually known), and cultural icons (Gombrich's criticism of the arrow).

B3. Voyager Golden Record (1977)

  • Wikipedia: Voyager Golden Record; PBS: the pulsar map
  • Summary: The cover reuses the pulsar map and hydrogen unit, adds binary playback instructions and a calibration image (a circle), and a uranium-238 clock.
  • Relevance: Borrow the calibration image before real images and the format instructions on the outermost layer. Avoid: analog dependence and pictorial conventions.

C. Transmitted digital messages, 1999 to today

C1. Cosmic Call 1 and 2 (1999, 2003): the Dutil–Dumas primer

  • Wikipedia: Cosmic Call; Mark Dominus: Cosmic Call primer, page 0; Michael Chorost: will extraterrestrials understand a message we send?; Plus Magazine
  • Summary: 23 pages of 127 × 127 bits, with borders and a 128-pixel run for sync. 5 × 7 glyphs were chosen for large Hamming distance between them, glyphs are spread over several lines to resist bursts, and page numbers are robust.
  • Relevance: Borrow minimum distance between symbol codes, interleaving, prime dimensions plus a sync marker, and a final page of questions. Known errors: two wrong "=" signs and a wrong neutron mass were transmitted. This is the strongest argument for automated truth checking. The framing was ambiguous (127 × 127 × 23 could also be read as 127 pages of 127 × 23), and reading direction was undefined.

C2. Teen Age Message (2001)

  • Wikipedia: Teen Age Message
  • Summary: A Doppler-compensated carrier, a theremin concert and binary images.
  • Relevance: The idea of a pre-carrier for frequency lock. Analog art carries no semantics.

C3. Sónar Calling GJ 273b (2017–2018), the design most similar to ours

  • Scientific American: new keys to help extraterrestrials unlock our messages; Universe Today: hello there, GJ 273b
  • Summary: 2-FSK. A "hello" of primes (up to 137 according to the project's site; one article says 193), then 8-bit words with a leading marker bit (10000001 = 1). Teaches counting → arithmetic → Pythagoras → trigonometry → sine waves that describe the carrier itself → sound → music. A "cosmic clock" changed with each transmission. Everything was sent three times ("whatever appears two out of three times").
  • Relevance: Borrow the marker bit in each word, the self-reference to the medium, and the cosmic clock as a built-in check. Improve on: plain triple repetition is costly and vulnerable to periodic bursts.

C4. A Message From Earth (2008) and Lone Signal (2013)

  • Universe Today: messages from Earth beamed to an alien world; Scientific American: good morning, Gliese 526
  • Relevance: Lone Signal split a rigorous primer channel (Busch's language) from a payload channel. Avoid: natural-language payloads with no key ("extrasolar spam").

C5. "A Beacon in the Galaxy" (2022)

  • Jiang et al., Galaxies 10:55. arXiv 2203.04288
  • Summary: An updated Arecibo-style message: mathematics → physics → biochemistry → location → images → invitation to reply. Never transmitted.
  • Relevance: The topic sequence matches our layers. Not tested for decodability.

C6. "The Last Arecibo Message" (2024)

  • Palencia-Torres et al., arXiv 2411.09790. arXiv 2411.09790
  • Summary: designed in 2020 and never sent: the telescope collapsed that December. Frequency shifts of 10 Hz around 2380 MHz at 20 bit/s, a 37 × 149 grid, identical pre-phase and end-phase (primes, π). No error correction.
  • Relevance: identical opening and closing phases. We send no terminator: our beacon is sent once at the start, and in repeated sending it separates the copies.

D. Decodability testing and decoding research

D1. SETI Decrypt Challenge (2016)

  • René Heller (paper 2017). arXiv 1706.00653
  • Summary: 1,902,341 bits = 7 × 359 × 757. More than 300 replies, 66 correct; about half were independent.
  • Relevance: Use specific graded questions. Isolate test subjects; for us, fresh LLM sessions without web access.

D2. A Sign in Space (2023–2024)

  • Space.com: alien signal from Mars decoded
  • Summary: About 5,000 participants extracted the signal in ~10 days. Decoding took ~1 year (Ken and Keli Chaffin), and the meaning is still disputed.
  • Relevance: Extraction, decoding and interpretation are separate stages and should be scored separately. Pictorial messages without taught semantics stay ambiguous.

D3. Matessa: decoding algorithms

  • Michael Matessa, "How to decode interstellar messages" (with Vakoch, Lower, Barkow and DeVito), Acta Astronautica 192 (2022) 77–81; and "Algorithms for Decoding Interstellar Messages", JBIS 75 (2022) 357–361.
  • ScienceDirect; Academia.edu: Algorithms for Decoding Interstellar Messages
  • Summary: Three stages: find the dimensions (helped by primes); classify symbols as delimiters, values, relations and functions; assign meaning with meta-algorithms that track hypotheses. Implemented in Lisp.
  • Relevance: Build our own symbolic, non-LLM decoder baseline. It has no training-data contamination.

D4. Zenil et al.: structure discovery without priors

  • arXiv 2303.16045; arXiv 2405.07803
  • Summary: Algorithmic-information methods find Arecibo's width of 23 with about 1.5 % of the bits wrong (3 % set at random); with more noise only after each bit is enlarged to a 6 × 6 block. (Press reports of "a quarter of the bits flipped" refer to the enlarged image.)
  • Relevance: A validator: the word width and raster widths must be recoverable after noise is added.

D5. LLM-based decoding tests

  • No published study found. Our LLM blind test, since run (see blind tests), appears to be novel. The survey recommended designing it against contamination: a randomised codeword mapping per trial, no English or ASCII leakage, and control messages that are scrambled or false.

E. Related fields

  • Nuclear waste warnings: Human Interference Task Force, Sebeok's "atomic priesthood", "ray cats" (Wikipedia: Ray cat), and the Sandia WIPP Markers Panel, SAND92-1382 (OSTI). Messages should be layered (rudimentary → complex), truthful and highly redundant, and should avoid culture-dependent symbols.
  • Rosetta Project (Rosetta Project: the disk): progressive magnification, where the outer layer teaches how to read the inner one, and parallel texts. For us: sometimes send the same statement in two representations as a cross-check.
  • Self-extracting formats and bootstrapping (background): TLV/ASN.1, self-extracting archives, compiler bootstraps (stage0/GNU Mes), "the emulator in the archive". The shared pattern is a tiny, verifiable core → interpreter → everything else.

G. Key lessons for our design

These are the survey's recommendations as written before the design was settled; what was adopted is in the specification.

  1. Beacon: a prime beacon at the start (the project later decided against a terminator: in repeated sending the beacon separates the copies), plus a fully predictable calibration block (word width, bit order, polarity). Validator: an autocorrelation detector must recover the word width at 10–25% bit errors.
  2. Codebook distance: guarantee a minimum Hamming distance between all symbol codes, not just per-word parity. Measure the probability of silently decoding to a different valid symbol.
  3. Interleave the copies: plain triple repetition fails against bursts that hit the same position in every copy. Permute or invert copies 2 and 3. Test bursts and periodic interference.
  4. Teach the error code later: the early layers must be readable while ignoring the check bits. Later, state the Hamming equations as checkable sentences. Optionally follow with Reed–Solomon whose decoder is written in DEF/λ.
  5. Arity must be explicit: encode it in the type bits, or use parentheses. The validator proves that every sentence has exactly one parse.
  6. Machine-check every statement, including significant figures against CODATA. Teach ≠ and false with explicit counterexamples.
  7. Explicit symbol introduction: a NEW marker plus ≥k examples before free use. The bootstrap validator enforces this.
  8. Symbolic decoder baseline (Matessa) with no LLM involved.
  9. LLM blind test: randomised mapping per trial, graded questions per layer, control messages, and separate scores for extraction, decoding and interpretation.
  10. Rasters: a calibration raster first, w and h given as numbers before each raster, no mixed notation, no arrows or cultural icons.
  11. Physical units: two independent anchors (hydrogen line, plus atom counts and phase changes of water), cross-linked with dimensionless constants, and a "cosmic clock" check.
  12. Pulsar map: ≥10 pulsars, periods in hydrogen units, period derivatives for dating, expressed in mathematical notation rather than a drawing.
  13. Chemistry: elements as proton counts first, molecules as graphs in the set layer, rasters last.
  14. Communication: Lincos-style Ha/Hb dialogues and a final "questions to you" section. Describe humans quantitatively.
  15. Self-description as a fixed point: a metacircular parser/evaluator in the message's own language, which the validator runs on the message itself.

I. From mathematics to physics, and to "us"

Second research report: how earlier projects made the leap from mathematics to the physical world and to the senders. Anything not confirmed by search or fetch is marked [unverified].

Summary

  • Most projects started physics in one of three places: the hydrogen atom (as dimensionless ratios, a spectral line, or the 21 cm spin-flip), the transmission itself (pulse durations, the carrier wavelength or frequency), or proton counts (atomic numbers).
  • Only one bridge was blind-tested and written up (Busch and Reddick, 2009). The decoder said the move from mathematics to physics was the hardest part, and everything after it was easy.
  • The two richest primers both ship false statements. Dutil–Dumas lists uranium-208, which does not exist, gets Avogadro's exponent wrong and has a typo in the neutron mass. The current CosmicOS source gives oxygen 16 protons instead of 8. So nobody before has managed to state physics without saying anything false. The way to do it is to write measured values as intervals with explicit bounds and to mark empirical statements as such. CosmicOS's assume marker and its float:= tolerance come closest.

I1. Project by project

Lincos (Freudenthal, 1960)

  • Structure of Part I: four chapters: (1) mathematics, (2) time, (3) behaviour/conversation, (4) mass, space and motion, which also touches human bodies and the Solar System. So Part I did reach physics. Part II (Matter, Earth, Life, Behaviour 2) was planned but never written. Sources: Wikipedia: Lincos language and Xenology, section 24.2.4
  • The bridge (chapter 2, "Time"): pulses of different lengths are sent, each followed by Sec and a number. The receiver sees that the number is always proportional to the measured duration, so Sec is a unit of time. The unit is never defined; it is shown through the medium (the receiver's own clock measures the pulse). The chapter then adds duration, moments in time, before/after, "occurs", wavelength and frequency.
  • Units beyond time: chapter 4 covers length, volume, speed of light and mass. How length and mass are anchored is [unverified]. The likely route is time plus the signal's own speed.
  • Uncertainty: none found. Pulse measurement is implicitly approximate, but nothing marks approximation. [unverified]
  • The path to "us" (Ha and Hb): chapter 3 stages little plays between actors Ha, Hb, Hc. Example: Ha Inq Hb ?x 2x=5; Hb answers 5/2; Ha says Ben (good). When Hb answers 10/4, Ha says Mal (bad): the answer is true but not in lowest terms. That is the key move. "Good/bad" is taught separately from Ver/Fal (true/false), so norms, intentions and conversation come in through observed behaviour around mathematical tasks. Meta-dialogue follows ("who asked Hb?" answered "Ha").
  • Criticisms: very long and demanding; a 1962 review notes it uses logic and linguistics terminology without defining it. It was never transmitted or tested.

Astraglossa (Hogben, 1952)

  • Numbers are sent as unary "dashes"; operators as "flashes" (radioglyphs). It opens with an "entrance examination" (I+II+III=VI), then new symbols, then affirmative, negative, question and "or" markers, then triangular numbers and trigonometry. Hogben said "astronomy will be the topic of our first factual conversations", with a later "star-lore" course. Sources: CosmicOS: Astraglossa and David Darling: Astraglossa
  • Anchoring: the sky both parties can see (the shared-sky strategy). The detail that the Sun, planets and their periods were identified by timing them in the message's own pulse units is [unverified]; the lecture text (reprinted in Science in Authority, 1963) could not be reached. It was never tested.

Arecibo message (1974)

  • The bridge: atomic numbers 1, 6, 7, 8, 15 (H, C, N, O, P), written as plain numbers. No physics is defined; elements are identified as integers, so the receiver must know atomic numbers already.
  • Chemistry and life: the nucleotides are written as atom counts in H-C-N-O-P order; then a DNA helix with a base-pair count (about 4.29×10⁹, now known to be wrong; the true figure is about 3.2×10⁹).
  • Units: the transmission's own wavelength, 126 mm. Human height = 14 × λ ≈ 1.76 m; dish diameter = 2430 × λ ≈ 306 m. Self-reference to the medium.
  • Path to us: the human figure, population (~4.3×10⁹, given without a timestamp), the Solar System with Earth offset, and the telescope.
  • Uncertainty: none; every value is a bare integer.
  • Criticisms: many prior assumptions (the 73×23 grid, a pictorial reading); the genome figure is wrong; it was a technology demonstration. Automated tests disagree on whether the frame can even be recovered. Source: Wikipedia: Arecibo message
  • Drake's informal test-decodings of his 1961 prototype (colleagues mostly failed to decode the whole thing) are [unverified].

Pioneer plaque (1972–73) and Voyager record cover (1977)

  • The bridge: a drawing of the hydrogen hyperfine (spin-flip) transition, with a "1" under it, defines one unit of length (21 cm) and one unit of time (about 0.7 ns).
  • Units in use: the woman's height is binary 8 → 8 × 21 cm ≈ 1.68 m. Pulsar periods are binary multiples of the hydrogen time unit. The Solar System diagram uses 1/10 of Mercury's orbit.
  • Pulsar map: 14 pulsars give both location and date, because pulsars slow down at known rates. One period was given to more digits than were actually known, a false-precision problem. Source: Wikipedia: Pioneer plaque
  • Voyager cover: the same hydrogen key; rotation (3.6 s) and playing time in hydrogen units; video decoding instructions; a calibration first image (a circle, to fix the aspect ratio); a uranium-238 sample as a physical clock (half-life 4.468×10⁹ yr) that measures elapsed time without any shared calendar. The record's images then go mathematics/physics → Solar System → DNA → anatomy → culture. Source: Wikipedia: Voyager Golden Record
  • Criticisms: pictures are conventions (Gombrich's arrow critique); gender and race depiction.
  • Takeaway: the hydrogen icon is clever but pictorial, and it depends on the reader knowing how humans draw atoms.

Cosmic Call 1999/2003 (Dutil–Dumas primer, 23 pages of 127×127 bitmaps)

Annotated by M. J. Dominus at Mark Dominus: the Cosmic Call primer (pages p00–p23 confirmed). Page topics: mathematics 1–5, physics 6–11 and 21, Earth 12–14 and 19–20, humans 15–18, cosmology 22, questions 23.

  • p03 (notation): scientific notation and powers of ten. √2 is written as 1.414… with a plain "=" and an ellipsis. There is no "approximately" symbol anywhere.

  • p06 (first physics, chemistry): pictures of hydrogen (p+e) and helium; glyphs for proton, neutron, electron, mass and charge. Then mp = 1836 me, qp = −qe, mn ≈ 1.00138 mp, qn = 0, and eight more elements by proton and neutron counts. Dominus: the dimensionless 1836 is the clearest anchor, while charges of 0/±1 "could mean many things".

  • p07 (mass): an isotope table, then the kilogram defined via the number of ¹²C atoms (Avogadro). Errors:

    • "U" with 92p/116n is uranium-208, which does not exist.
    • Gold-196 and zinc-65 are odd, radioactive choices.
    • Avogadro's number is written as 6022137×10ⁿ with the wrong exponent and grams/kilograms confused, making the kilogram about 90% too small.
    • The neutron mass has a typo.

    The particle masses in kg on the same page contradict the faulty definition, so redundancy would expose the error.

  • p08 (time and space): hydrogen energy levels drawn with ground = 0 and continuum = 1, so the levels sit at 3/4, 8/9, 15/16 (Rydberg, "approximately"). Hα is given as 656.2852 nm and 456.8021 THz; their product ≈ 299 792 458 defines c, and the metre and hertz. The second is 1/Hz. The receiver can check λ·ν = c.

  • p09: derived units (N, J, Pa, W, velocity, acceleration), Planck's constant h, gravitational constant G. Notation for inverse units is inconsistent.

  • p10 (temperature): a water heating curve with 273 K / 373 K at 101 300 Pa. Criticisms: the kelvin was then defined by the triple point of water (until 2019), not by standard pressure; the x-axis is labelled "time" when it should be energy; Earth's atmospheric pressure is assumed known.

  • p11–14: Sun and Jupiter; Earth–Moon; year, month and day; ages of Sun and Earth; terrain and atmosphere composition; g.

  • p15–18 (us): a human figure (borrowed from Pioneer) with height; vital statistics (population "around 6,000,000,000", life 70 yr, 80 kg, 311 K, hearing 20 Hz–20 kHz, eye pigment peaks); DNA bases; cell respiration and division (~10¹³ cells). Criticisms: sex-averaged values; 311 K should be 310 K; the sound diagram draws transverse waves (sound is compressional); Dominus was "completely mystified" by the hearing section.

  • p21 (self-reference to the medium): a transmitter drawing; the carrier is 5 010 240 000 Hz and 0.059836 m; message size 127×127×23.

  • p23: questions for the recipients; glyph meanings are unclear.

  • Uncertainty overall: essentially none. Single point values, "=" used for approximations, and "around" only in the prose description of the population.

  • Decodability: Dominus decoded it in "a happy couple of hours" (as a human who already knows what humans are). In Busch's test the format itself was "trivial" to crack, but that says nothing about the semantics.

Sónar Calling GJ 273b (2017; METI tutorial by Vakoch, Matessa and others)

  • Approach: "We explain time through pulses that have a clearly defined duration." Radio frequency is explained by pointing at the two frequencies the transmission actually uses. The mathematics climbs counting → arithmetic → Pythagorean triples → trigonometry → division → sine waves → radio waves themselves. Each day's tutorial was sent three times so the receiver can take a 2-of-3 vote.
  • Path to us: then the transmitter becomes "a musical instrument": tones at mathematically precise intervals introduce melody, followed by 10-second music pieces.
  • Principle: the form of the signal demonstrates its content (Vakoch's semiotic, iconic idea).
  • Source: Scientific American: new keys to help extraterrestrials unlock our messages (carrier details [unverified]).

DeVito & Oehrle (JBIS 43:561–568, 1990)

  • Approach: numerals and logic → chemistry → physics. It assumes both sides "recognize the chemical elements and the periodic table", "know enough chemistry to carry out chemical calculations", and "have made a quantitative study of the states of matter". With that, they say one can communicate the gram, calorie, kelvin and pressure units.
  • Units: units are learned as your units through shared stoichiometry and phase transitions; nothing is assumed to be universal.
  • Gaps: the exact construction (gram via atomic weights and the mole; temperature via water's melting and boiling; calorie via heating water) is [unverified]; the paper could not be reached.
  • Criticism: it assumes 19th-century-style human chemistry.
  • Sources: Space.com: making contact with aliens through language and math and Wikipedia: Communication with extraterrestrial intelligence

Busch's language (2009 blind test; Lone Signal 2013)

  • Format: an 8-quad-word symbolic language (delimiters, =, ≠, integers, variables, floating point, e, π, i, quadratic formula). It is the closest relative of our design.
  • The bridge: dimensionless constants (fine-structure constant α, gravitational coupling constant, mp/me, mn/me), then Planck units defined by formulas relating them. The proton, neutron and electron are nouns defined by property bundles (mass, charge, charge radius). Then a chart of nuclides with masses and proton, neutron and electron counts, plus hydrogen's radius, binding energies and spectral lines. Then molecules.
  • Path to us, entirely by definite descriptions:
    • The Sun is a noun with mass 1.989e30 kg, 5778 K, radius 6.955e8 m, power 3.846e26 W and an isotopic composition. The receiver can check all of this against the star the signal comes from.
    • The planets come with masses, radii and Kepler-consistent orbits.
    • Earth is the planet with a rocky composition and an oxygen-rich atmosphere (chemical disequilibrium, implying life) whose orbit matches the beacon's Doppler shift (self-reference to the medium).
  • Results: the decoder (Reddick) succeeded in about 12 hours. She reported that the move from mathematics to physics was the hardest part; after the units, the rest was "readily understandable". She short-cut the chemistry by recognising H₂, H₂O and O₂ formulas. Three of five undergraduates found the delimiters within an hour.
  • Uncertainty: values are given as floating point with no error bars, so precision is only implicit.
  • Source: arXiv 0911.3976 (full text read). Lone Signal (2013, to Gliese 526) reused the language: hydrogen atom, periodic table, location. Lone Signal's specific content is [unverified].

CosmicOS (Paul Fitzpatrick)

  • Path: lambda-calculus mathematics → π, e, i, Euler → Turing machine → objects → a simulated world of rooms, doors and an agent → section X, "Physics", which covers particles and elements and then logic gates, a cellular signal field, oscillators and an SR latch → a clocked World with an autonomous agent. Sources: CosmicOS and GitHub: paulfitz/cosmicos
  • Physics section (msg/COS_Element.js, read directly):
    • Absolute particle masses are wrapped in assume and are, in the source's own words, "not included in the message, since they are unit-specific. The message will only present ratios."
    • Statements like float:= (proton mass) (* (electron mass) 1836.15267) follow, with mn/mp = 1.0013784 and charges ±1/0.
    • Then atom built from Z and A; elements with isotope lists; molecules (H₂, O₂, O₃, H₂O, NH₃, CH₄ …) as count statements.
    • The section credits Busch's DearET message as inspiration.
  • Approximate equality (COS_Series.js): float:= means "within (x+y)/200000", a stated tolerance. This is how "≈" is made checkable.
  • The assume marker: an identity function in the evaluator. It flags empirical content without changing what is computed.
  • Bug found: define oxygen | elemental new 16 | vector 16 17 18 gives oxygen 16 protons; it should be 8. Machine checking caught nothing because the statement is consistent, not true.
  • Physical world as simulation: physics arrives as executable models (gates, fields, worlds), not as references to measured things.
  • The current site wraps the message in a fictional decoding diary; it states the message was "never sent".

McConnell (ACETI; Beyond Contact, 2001)

  • Messages are programs for a defined virtual machine. Physics is conveyed as numerical simulation: an N-body Solar System model of a few kB, with initial conditions at about 50 bytes per body (64-bit position and velocity). The receiver checks the program's output against what it observes.
  • Interleaving occasional uncompressed images is suggested.
  • Source: Centauri Dreams

I4. Lessons for our design

  1. Introduce physical things as uninterpreted constants, fixed by descriptions. Busch's Sun and Earth are the model: the noun is the bundle of properties the receiver can check. "Hydrogen" then means whatever satisfies all the true statements made about it, which matches the "identify, not define" framing exactly.

  2. Make empirical statements a distinct, marked kind of statement. CosmicOS's assume points the way. The checker can verify consistency and arithmetic, never truth about the world. A marker such as (observed …) lets the receiver tell proven facts from reported ones, and lets the build treat them as axioms of a theory rather than theorems.

  3. "Never false" means intervals, not "=". Every earlier project wrote approximations with "=" (Dutil–Dumas's √2 = 1.414…, Last Arecibo's π = 52163/16604). Write measured values as ℚ bounds wide enough to stay true under CODATA uncertainty and future revisions. Bounds are exactly checkable with ℚ and <. This also teaches that measurement is uncertain.

  4. Lead with the exact mathematical fingerprint. Hydrogen levels 1 − 1/n² (giving 3/4, 8/9, 15/16 …) and line ratios like (1/4 − 1/9)/(1 − 1/4) = 5/27 are exact rationals that can be stated and proved. Then add one observed statement: "the bound on hydrogen's measured line ratio contains 5/27". Use mp/me and α only as bounded fingerprints. Do not rely on Planck-unit gymnastics: Busch's decoder found that step hardest.

  5. Take absolute scale from the medium, then tie it to hydrogen. The message still travels as a physical signal, so tick can be the bit period and carrier the transmitted frequency (Lincos, Arecibo, Sónar). Then give bounded ratios such as carrier/21 cm-line frequency or tick × Hα frequency. That produces two independent anchors that cross-check each other. Avoid relying on a 21 cm picture (Pioneer) or on SI names. Plan for both anchors to be needed, because a relayed or re-encoded copy loses the medium.

  6. Add exact counting chemistry early: it is truth-safe and reaches biology. Proton and neutron counts and molecular formulas are integers, so statements about them are exactly true. Statements of the form (H₂O has 2 H and 1 O; DNA bases as atom counts) are what let Arecibo reach DNA in a few lines. Tuples/records and finite sets are needed first.

  7. Build redundancy that lets the receiver, and the build, catch errors. Uranium-208, Avogadro's exponent, Arecibo's genome figure and CosmicOS's oxygen-with-16-protons all passed review or machine checks because nothing contradicted them. Include cross-relations that must hold: λ·ν within the bounds for c; atomic mass ≈ Z·mp + N·mn − binding energy, within bounds; Kepler's third law for orbits. Generate all empirical bounds automatically from a reference dataset (CODATA, the nuclide tables) during the build rather than typing them by hand.

  8. Timestamp anything that changes. Population, Earth's rotation, pulsar periods and "now" all drift. Phrase such statements relative to an epoch the message defines, with bounds, and note that values change over time. Voyager's U-238 and Pioneer's pulsar spin-down show the value of a physical clock. In symbols, the hydrogen-referenced tick plus a stated count since a defined event is the analogue.

  9. Use simulation as a supplement, not as the reference. A program (an N-body orbit, a cellular field) shows that a law generates observations (McConnell, CosmicOS). Pair each model with a description that picks out the real system ("the star this signal comes from has mass within …", "the planet whose orbit fits the Doppler shift of this carrier"). A model alone says "a world like this", not "this world".

  10. Reach "us" through behaviour and description, not pictures. The proven symbolic routes are:

    • Lincos's Ha/Hb dialogues, where "Mal" for the true-but-unreduced 10/4 separates norms from truth (essential before civilization);
    • description by property bundle (Busch's Earth: oxygen disequilibrium, Doppler match);
    • exact chemistry (DNA bases as counts);
    • bounded vital statistics (height in carrier wavelengths, population with an epoch).

    Order: particles → hydrogen → elements → molecules → Sun → planets → Earth → life chemistry → us → dialogue.

Caveats: the Dutil–Dumas pages, the Busch paper, the CosmicOS source and the Last Arecibo paper were confirmed in detail. The Lincos chapter-4 mechanics, Hogben's astronomy lessons, DeVito–Oehrle's unit constructions and Beacon in the Galaxy's page-level physics come from secondary sources or memory and are marked [unverified]. The primary texts (Freudenthal 1960; Science in Authority; JBIS 43; the Beacon PDF) would resolve them.

J. Agents, norms and peaceful intent: Lincos and Vakoch

Third research report: how Lincos teaches behaviour and conversation, what Vakoch and others proposed for communicating altruism and reciprocity, and how sent messages tried to signal peaceful intent. Freudenthal's book itself could not be retrieved (the Monoskop page returned 403), so the Lincos symbols come from secondary sources, mainly Latronico (2008), who quotes actual Lincos dialogues. Vakoch's Acta Astronautica abstracts were partly paywalled; details come from abstract excerpts and interviews. Unconfirmed claims are marked [unverified].

J1. Lincos, Chapter 3 ("Behaviour")

Freudenthal's philosophy

  • Two ostensive anchors only. Without a shared language you can point only at numbers (counted pulses that "both signify and represent their significance", §1.01) and time (pulses of stated length in seconds, then a clock). Every other concept is defined inside the language and traced back to these two anchors. He calls the number signs "ideophonetic" (§0.17). (Latronico.)
  • Quasi-general definitions (§1.00). A concept is taught by giving (a) a few instances, (b) rules or properties of the concept, and (c) at least one non-instance. The receiver is expected to generalise to "the proposition that is intended by the program text". Latronico points out that this is incomplete induction. It works only if the receiver assumes the sender meant something, and assumes the receiver will make the same assumption.
  • Mathematics is not "the universal truth". It comes first only because it is the one subject that can be taught by pointing. The aim is a language for "people who know what they say, and who endeavour to utter meaningful speech" (§1.31).
  • Lincos is for dialogue, not only tautologies. It deliberately allows nonsense, contradiction and suspension of bivalence, because real conversation needs them. Its "i" (iota) definite-description operator may fail to refer.
  • Structure. Part I (1960) has four chapters: Mathematics, Time, Behaviour, Space. A planned Part II (Matter, Earth, Life, Behaviour 2) was never written.

How Chapter 3 works

  • Actors are staged "plays". Speakers named Ha, Hb, Hc, … first appear only as sources of propositions: symbols declared not to be numbers, which exchange utterances. Memory, will, personality and body are attributed to them only later.
  • Utterances are named by their time interval. Every utterance occupies a unique clock interval t1 t2. A speaker can then refer to "the event in t1..t2" with Fit ("happens / occurs"), as in iz…t1t2 Fit z, meaning "the event that occurred between t1 and t2". This is Lincos's main device for mention versus use and for talking about statements. Once the receiver has grasped the idea, the explicit time stamps are dropped.
  • "Inq" means "says", not "inquires". Latronico glosses Inq as the predicate for "saying", from Latin inquit. So Ha Inq Hb. p means "Ha says to Hb: p". Questions are marked by the operator ?x, not by Inq. Popular sources that gloss Inq as "inquiry" are misleading.
  • Questions are introduced first in Chapter 1. There they work like quantifiers: ?x…x = 1+10 …… x = 11, with pauses separating question and answer. Questions become real conversational acts only in Chapter 3.
  • The chapter builds up to a definition of human life, the human subject, and "a sort of simplified formal ethical system" (Latronico).

Chapter 4 (brief)

Chapter 4 introduces space through delayed speech. Speakers notice lags in their dialogue, positions are introduced as one-dimensional numbers, and lag is shown to be proportional to distance. It then builds up to 3-D geometry, mechanics, waves, the speed of light, mass, human physical features and the Solar System. Latronico calls this "a description, not a definition" of space.

Commentary and criticism

  • Latronico (2008) is the most useful critique.
    • Lincos depends on the receiver assuming the sender had an intention, not on formal rigour.
    • Key steps (Ani, Agg, Ben/Mal) contradict earlier semantic postulates on purpose, and work only if the receiver revises its beliefs charitably.
    • Ethics in Lincos presupposes that the receiver already knows conversational norms. His verdict is that Lincos is "not a didactics and not even an encyclopedia, but a mere vocabulary": it names concepts the receiver must already have.
  • Other reviews: Kneebone (Journal of Symbolic Logic, 1969), Dawson (Mind, 1964), British Journal for the Philosophy of Science (1962), Narens (Journal of Symbolic Logic, 1973). Not read.
  • xenology.info raises the standard objections: Gödel, different senses, possibly non-shared logic.
  • Ollongren built a second-generation Lingua Cosmica on constructive logic, where all expressions are verifiable (Astrolinguistics, Springer 2013). His chapter in Extraterrestrial Altruism (ch. 16) argues that both Lincos and New Lincos can express human morality and altruism, "even if the necessary encoding might not be perspicuous in all respects".
  • CosmicOS (Paul Fitzpatrick) is inspired by Lincos but aimed at programs (lambda calculus) rather than conversation. Its later sections build objects, a room-and-door world, and autonomous agents with rules, including one that "breaks its own rule". It has no dialogue protocol.

J2. Vakoch and related work

Vakoch & Matessa (2011), "An algorithmic approach to communicating reciprocal altruism in interstellar messages: Drawing analogies between social and astrophysical phenomena". Acta Astronautica 68(3–4): 459–475, doi:10.1016/j.actaastro.2009.12.012.

  • They model reciprocal altruism (short-term self-sacrifice for long-term benefit) through a stellar mass-loss analogy, scaled up to human interaction.
  • They present an adaptation of the Prisoner's Dilemma that shows the limits of pure cooperation and pure defection, argue that flexible strategies keep populations stable, and end by modelling Tit for Tat.
  • Vakoch has said in interview (Greater Good, 2008) that colleagues proposed sending ET computer programs that simulate this. In them, "actors" exist while they cooperate, and if they do not cooperate "they lose mass, until they ultimately disappear, or 'die'".
  • Rationale: reciprocity fits a message because "we'd hope to see our message reciprocated with a reply".

Kin selection. Vakoch: "We are looking at things like kin selection and reciprocal altruism". He proposed that later messages show parent–child relationships and how fractions of genetic material pass between generations. Explicit use of Hamilton's rule rB > C is [unverified].

"Constructing messages to extraterrestrials: an exosemiotic perspective" (Acta Astronautica 1998, doi:10.1016/S0094-5765(98)00029-0). Applies Peircean semiotics (icon, index, symbol) and argues for messages that build interpretive context step by step, with signs that resemble or point to what they mean before arbitrary symbols are used [unverified; abstract not retrieved].

"The dialogic model: representing human diversity in messages to extraterrestrials" (Acta Astronautica 42, 1998, 705–710, doi:10.1016/S0094-5765(98)00030-7). A single reply should preserve differences between human perspectives, even irreconcilable ones: it helps us look past our own assumptions, gives recipients more than one way to understand us, and shows the range of human concerns. It also argues for drafting replies now, before any detection.

"Communicating scientifically formulated spiritual principles in interstellar messages" (c. 2000). Proposes expressing values or spiritual principles through scientific and mathematical structure rather than doctrine [unverified details].

Common pattern: peaceful intent has always been asserted in natural language, gesture or imagery. That is the least decodable layer, and it is usually paired with location data. The decodable content has no intent layer at all.

J4. Lessons for a truth-only symbolic section on agents, norms and peace

  1. Name utterances as events, and quote rather than assert. Introduce (says A B (quote p)) with each utterance as an indexed event. The truth-only rule then holds even when a scene contains a false answer: (says hb ha (quote (= x 1/4))) is a true statement about the scene, while (= x 1/4) is never written bare.
  2. Make agents things whose behaviour can be checked: executable or explicitly hypothetical. Either define agents as programs whose runs the message computes (as in CosmicOS), or mark scenes as (scene k …), (model …) or (suppose …) contexts. Never make claims about real humans' conversations unless they are marked observed.
  3. Introduce agents minimally and in Freudenthal's order. Sources of utterances, questions and answers, evaluation of answers, knowing (told or perceived), wanting, possibility and permission, promise. A question can reuse the quantifier machinery: an open term with a hole; an answer is a binding.
  4. Put good/bad on acts and true/false on propositions, as different types. Teach good and bad as predicates over events such as answers, with a scene where the two judgements come apart (100+1 versus 101, or 10/4 versus 5/2).
  5. Use positive and negative examples, and expect the receiver to need conversational norms already (Latronico). Follow instances, rules, non-instance for each normative term. The four kinds of Mal (false, uninformative, unperspicuous, unsolicited) give natural contrast sets. Keep norms few and operational.
  6. Define harm and benefit in physics already taught. Vakoch and Matessa used mass loss: an agent "dies" when its mass reaches zero. Define benefit and harm as changes in a resource or survival variable of an agent, to avoid importing human values (Ruse).
  7. Show cooperation as mathematics, not as a claim. A payoff matrix with exact integers, strategies as functions (always cooperate, always defect, Tit for Tat, a generous variant), and the computed outcome of an iterated tournament or population dynamics.
  8. Let the message's own conduct be the evidence. The transmission is a costly gift of knowledge with no request attached; in Tit for Tat terms it is the opening cooperative move (Langston's and Harrison's framing). A patient, informative, perspicuous teaching posture also demonstrates good conduct by its own rules.
  9. State intent as a scoped, honest self-report, following Vakoch's dialogic model. "We come in peace" cannot be verified and over-claims for all of humanity. Truthful forms: "the senders of this message intend no harm", "the senders choose cooperate as first move", and an admission of diversity ("not all humans agree on all things"), which makes the peace claim more credible. Define honest operationally first: an agent all of whose says events quote true propositions.
  10. Treat promises as commitment plus visible fulfilment. Teach promise with a scene in which an agent says it will do X later and then does X. Promises about ourselves should be small and fulfillable inside the message ("after section k, we answer question q"), giving a demonstrable track record.
  11. Avoid looking weak or threatening. A stated retaliation rule can read as a threat; generous Tit for Tat shows conditional cooperation without menace. Do not describe capabilities, weapons or power, and do not beg or invite visits. Raybeck (predator–prey) and Brin argue that over-eager signalling invites exploitation: keep the tone of peer reciprocity.
  12. Keep peace content separate from location, and make questions to the receiver well-formed. Earlier messages paired goodwill with location. Unlike Cosmic Call p. 23, give every question an answer template, ask about shared mathematics and physics rather than their planet, keep one glyph to one meaning, and show a sample question answered first.

Process recommendation: test the section on naive decoders before finalising. Latronico's main warning is that each normative step depends on the receiver charitably revising its beliefs; testing shows where that charity breaks.
