Topics

The lessons by topic. The message itself is one sequence; the topics only group its lessons for reading, and a lesson can belong to more than one. Each section here is also the heading and introduction a topic gets on the other pages.

The signal

The message is designed for a receiver who knows nothing about us, apart from the fact that counting and physics work the same everywhere. That's why it never assumes a convention. Every convention is either easy to spot in the bitstream or taught with many examples before it is used.

It's built in layers: bits, then chunks, then tokens, then sentences, then lessons. You learn one layer at a time, in the same order as the receiver.

The first lessons show how the message is read, from the bottom up: bits and the beacon, then chunks, tokens and calibration. Sentences in brackets come with the first lesson that uses them, and the last lesson shows how the message ends.

In every lesson, the code blocks are the message itself, exactly as it is sent. Blocks marked as illustrations only explain and are not sent.

Mathematics

A lesson introduces one new concept with many examples. There's no "new lesson" marker: the receiver sees the change in what is being talked about. Some lessons are complete, such as counting to 255; others will get more examples.

The message repeats itself on purpose. A new symbol is used in at least three sentences of its first lesson, usually many more than are needed to pin it down, and some things are said twice in different ways: a law after its instances, a definition in two forms, a number reached by two routes. The receiver can then check one reading against another, and a sentence damaged on the way does less harm.

Logic

Truth values, variables and quantifiers, and then sentences about sentences: quoting a sentence without claiming it, and computing the value of an expression with eval.

Physics

Until now, everything in the message could be proved from the message itself. From here on it talks about things outside the message: particles, atoms, light. These cannot be defined, only identified. The message states facts that only one thing in the receiver's own physics can satisfy.

Two things change. Measured facts are marked with observed, so the receiver can tell them from proved ones. And measured values are never written with =: they are written as ranges with <, wide enough to include the uncertainty of the measurement.

Chemistry

How electrons fill the shells of atoms, why some elements hardly react, and how atoms join into molecules, among them the four bases of DNA.

Astronomy

What a star is, made from the physics before it: a body held together by gravity, shining as a black body, made mostly of hydrogen and helium, with other bodies going round it. Then one star in particular. The lesson on that star describes where the message comes from, and it can be left out of a message: no other lesson depends on it.

Games

Two players each choose, at the same time, to cooperate or to defect. Each loses least by defecting, yet both lose less if both cooperate. This is the prisoner's dilemma, and it is pure mathematics: the rules, the strategies and their results can all be computed.

The minimal version

The lessons that are needed to state the result of the game, and no others: the signal, arithmetic, truth values, lists, quantifiers, sequences, chance, if, and the prisoner's dilemma. They form the minimal version of the message: no lesson in it uses a symbol that a lesson outside it introduces, and the build checks that.
