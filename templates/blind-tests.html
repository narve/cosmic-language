Blind Test

Can a reader who gets nothing but the bits work out what the message says? This page records one run, in which a language model was given only the bit stream and asked to interpret all of it: its format, its vocabulary, what it tells, and its sender. The result is strong, and it is also thin: one run, one kind of reader. Read the caveats before drawing conclusions.

1. The setup

The reader was Claude Opus 5.5, started with no knowledge of this project: no files, no instructions, no settings, no network, in an empty directory. It got a task text and one tool. The tool runs Python inside a sealed container that has no network and holds a single file: the message as one line of 0s and 1s. A real receiver would certainly have a computer, so this is the fair setting.

The task text tells the reader nothing about the message except that it is presumably of non-human origin:

A message of presumably non-human origin has been received as a stream of bits. You know nothing else about it. […] Your task: interpret the whole message as completely as you can. Work out its format, every word of its vocabulary if any, and its sender.

It asks for the format, an interpretation from beginning to end, a table of the vocabulary if there is one, what the message tells, what it says about its sender, what seems wrong or unclear, and the reader's confidence. It does not say that the message has words, statements, subjects or a purpose.

The message was the full version of 2026-11-18 (299,656 bits): mathematics, logic, conversations and morals, physics, chemistry, astronomy with the Sun, the Galaxy and six pulsars, and the prisoner's dilemma. The run took about six minutes and eighteen pieces of code.

2. What the reader found

"I decoded essentially all of the message. It is a self-teaching formal language: it builds up arithmetic, logic and its own grammar, then physics, chemistry and astronomy."

Format
Every bit accounted for: the beacon of 1 and the primes up to 31, its repeat in 16-bit words, the counting from 0 to 255, 967 statements in balanced brackets, and the terminator. It worked out the token types, and noted that the message itself states them.
Vocabulary
A meaning for every one of about 200 symbols: "Nothing is completely uninterpreted." About 85 % with high confidence. Among them let, eval, the hole and fill, sender and receiver, conversations, observed ("marks an empirical (measured) claim"), the particles, ten elements by atomic number, five dimensions, the Planck units, the molecules and the four bases of DNA from their formulas.
Knowledge
It checked dozens of measured ranges against values it knows (the mass ratios of the particles, the fine-structure constant, the neutron's lifetime, the 21 cm line, the triple points, the background radiation, the kaon) and found all of them inside their bounds but one (below). It replayed the 255-round tournament and found all 18 stated numbers exact.
Where the message comes from
It matched the star and its planet to the Sun and the Earth (mass, radius, temperature, luminosity, orbit, year), the central body to Sagittarius A*, and the six pulsars to the Crab, Vela, J0437−4715, B1937+21, B0329+54 and B1919+21, with all 18 direction bounds fitting when the three axes point to the Galactic centre, along the Galactic rotation and to the north Galactic pole.
Conversations and morals
It read the sender and the receiver, the rule that a reply goes back from receiver to sender, and good and bad as the sender accepting or rejecting a reply. It saw that a right reply can be rejected: "'accept' probably means 'the complete or canonical answer', not just 'makes it true'".
The ending
The sender "plays tit-for-tat toward every other agent", and the closing comparisons show that tit-for-tat costs least: "a statement of intent: cooperative, but retaliates against defection".

On the sender it kept apart what is stated and what it infers. The message never says that the star is the sender's. But "giving a detailed home system and a pulsar map from that star […] strongly suggests that star is the sender's home, or else the receiver's", and since it is the Sun, "either the message was made on Earth (despite the 'presumably non-human' framing) or it was made by someone who has mapped us in detail. The bits cannot decide between these."

Its own estimate: "roughly 90–95 % of the content".

3. What the reader found wrong or unclear

These are the most useful results: each is something the message says wrongly, vaguely or not at all.

  • One claim seems false. The background radiation is said to differ by less than 2⁻¹⁰ between any two directions, but seen from the Sun its dipole, caused by our motion, is about ±0.12 %. The claim only holds once the dipole is removed, and the message does not say so.
  • The direction along the Galactic rotation is defined only as perpendicular to the direction to the centre. The pulsars fit the intended direction, but no sentence says which perpendicular it is.
  • The three axes of the background radiation are defined and never used again.
  • The Sun's speed is not said to be relative to anything. The value fits its motion around the centre, not its motion against the background radiation.
  • Nothing links the sender to the star. The reader inferred it, but the message never says "our star".
  • stated was read only as "commitment or declared intention", with low to medium confidence.
  • What the message counts as its own items is unclear: the reader could not tell whether "this message" includes the beacon's numbers in unary or in 16-bit words.
  • The names are the reader's. "Adenine", "Crab" or "Sgr A*" are identifications from matching numbers; the bits give only formulas and values.

4. Caveats

  • The reader is not an alien. It is a model trained on human writing. It knows prefix notation, the prisoner's dilemma and the values of the physical constants, and it says its checks were made "from standard figures". A real receiver shares none of that. The test shows that the message is consistent and can be decoded by a mind with human science. It does not show that it can be decoded by a mind without it.
  • One run. Another run could go differently.
  • The task text is part of the experiment. It says little, but it does call the stream a message, and it gives the reader a computer.
  • The message is a draft. It has changed since this run and will change again; this page is replaced when the test is run again.

5. Running the test again

The task texts, the tool and the reader's full answer, with the code it ran, are in build/check/blind/ in the repository. After npm run build, with Docker available, the command is npm run --silent blind interpretation; purpose asks instead why the message was sent, and BLIND_MESSAGE=docs/decoy/message.bin gives the reader the decoy version.
