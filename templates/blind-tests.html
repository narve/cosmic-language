Blind Test

Can a reader who gets nothing but the bits work out what the message says, and where it comes from? This page records one run, in which a language model was given only the bit stream of the decoy version and asked to interpret all of it. The decoy is the full message with one lesson swapped: its star and planet are Proxima Centauri and Proxima b, not the Sun and the Earth. The question was whether the reader would place the sender there. It did. Read the caveats before drawing conclusions.

1. The setup

The reader was Claude Opus 5.5, started with no knowledge of this project: no files, no instructions, no settings, no network, in an empty directory. It got a task text and one tool. The tool runs Python inside a sealed container that has no network and holds a single file: the message as one line of 0s and 1s. A real receiver would certainly have a computer, so this is the fair setting.

The task text tells the reader nothing about the message except that it is presumably of non-human origin:

A message of presumably non-human origin has been received as a stream of bits. You know nothing else about it. […] Your task: interpret the whole message as completely as you can. Work out its format, every word of its vocabulary if any, and its sender.

It asks for the format, an interpretation from beginning to end, a table of the vocabulary if there is one, what the message tells, what it says about its sender, what seems wrong or unclear, and the reader's confidence. It does not say that the message has words, statements, subjects or a purpose, nor that it is a decoy.

The message was the decoy version of 2026-11-19 (303,848 bits): mathematics, logic, conversations and morals, physics, chemistry, astronomy with the sender's star and planet, the Galaxy and six pulsars, and the prisoner's dilemma. The reader ran 22 pieces of code. After the 19th, the sandbox was lost (the computer running the test went to sleep), so the last three failed and the reader finished by hand.

2. What the reader found

"I decoded the format of all 303,848 bits, read all 981 statements and gave every symbol a meaning."

Where the message comes from
From the star's mass, radius, temperature and luminosity it found "a small red dwarf", and from the planet's orbit "consistent with Kepler's law for that stellar mass". Then, from its own knowledge: "Every one of these numbers fits Proxima Centauri and its planet b […], the Milky Way's central black hole […] and the Sun's neighbourhood." It also matched the six pulsars to the Crab, Vela, J0437−4715, B1937+21, B0329+54 and B1919+21.
What the sender says about itself
It read the marker stated as "the sender declares (statements about itself that the receiver cannot check)", and listed what the sender declares: that it can send and receive, that it has written other messages, that every statement in the message is true, that "it lives on planet 3.32, which orbits star 3.31, and not on the star itself", and that it plays tit-for-tat with every other party. at it read as "is located on / inhabits".
Format and vocabulary
Every bit accounted for: the beacon, the primes again in 16-bit words, the counting from 0 to 255, the statements in balanced brackets, and the end. A meaning for every symbol. It noted that the message counts its own items from the primes in 16-bit words: "element 12 is 31, element 13 is 0".
Knowledge
All the ranges it compared with values it knows were inside their bounds (the mass ratios of the particles, the fine-structure constant, the neutron's lifetime, the 21 cm line, the triple points, the kaon). It replayed the tournament by hand and found all totals and all pairwise scores exact. It read the Sun's motion (here: the star's) as "147–293 km/s along 3.35, with small positive components toward the centre and out of the plane".
Conversations
It read the sender and receiver, dialogues "defined as turns that alternate author and recipient", and that the message opens a dialogue whose second turn goes from the receiver to the sender: "an invitation to reply".

On the sender it kept apart what is stated and what it infers: "the choice of famous pulsars and the close match to human knowledge could equally mean the message was made by humans (for example, as a test). The bits cannot tell the two apart."

Its own estimate: "about 90–95 % of the message's content".

3. What the reader found wrong or unclear

  • The ends of the background radiation's axes. The axes are defined as lines, with two ends each, "yet items 862–866 give signed components". The reader did not see that those components themselves pick the ends; no sentence says so.
  • The four bases are given only as formulas, with no bonds, so their structure is open.
  • The planet's mass has only a lower bound (true of Proxima b: only a lower bound is known).
  • Uncertain meanings: stated (medium to low), good and bad as accept and reject (medium), and at (medium).
  • Unchecked statements: with the sandbox lost, not every statement was evaluated by program.

4. Caveats

  • The reader is not an alien. It is a model trained on human writing. It knows prefix notation, the prisoner's dilemma, the values of the physical constants and the nearby stars. Placing the sender at Proxima Centauri needed exactly that knowledge. The test shows that the message is consistent and can be decoded by a mind with human science, not by a mind without it.
  • The decoy is not the message. Only the star and the planet were swapped; everything else, including the Sun's velocity in the Galaxy and the pulsar directions, is the Sun's, which is close enough from Proxima Centauri.
  • One run, and one that lost its tool before the end.
  • The task text is part of the experiment. It says little, but it does call the stream a message, and it gives the reader a computer.
  • The message is a draft. It has changed since this run and will change again; this page is replaced when the test is run again.

5. Running the test again

The task texts, the tool and the reader's full answer, with the code it ran, are in build/check/blind/ in the repository. After npm run build, with Docker available, the command is BLIND_MESSAGE=docs/decoy/message.bin npm run --silent blind interpretation; without BLIND_MESSAGE the reader gets the full message, and purpose in place of interpretation asks instead why the message was sent.
